# Documented real-world collapses of block-built structures usable as rigid-block statics validation cases

Scope note: every case below is web-sourced; nothing comes from any local project. Imperial figures in the sources are converted to SI inline and marked "(converted)". Where a mechanism is reconstructed by investigators rather than observed, that is stated. Where only an abstract or a search snippet (not the full text) was readable, that is stated in the Gaps. Cases driven by material deterioration (Pavia, Noto, St Mark's) are kept only as *negative controls* — things a rigid-block engine should NOT be expected to reproduce — because a reader will otherwise ask why they are missing.

Shorthand used throughout for the rigid-block checks: a free-standing rigid wall of thickness *t* and height *h* on a rigid base overturns under a uniform horizontal body acceleration a ≥ g·t/h, under a uniform pressure p ≥ γ·t²/h (γ = unit weight), and under a point force at the top F ≥ W·t/(2h) (W = weight). Those are my derivations, not sourced claims; they are used in the Inferences sections.

## KQ1. Masonry free-standing wall collapses with investigation reports (HSE / NIOSH FACE / FAI), and the height-to-thickness guidance used to judge them

### Takeaway
The best-instrumented free-standing-wall collapse found is the Liberton High School privacy wall (Scottish Fatal Accident Inquiry 2017): a 3.65 m × 2.3 m × 105 mm brick wall, 1865 kg, fully cracked at its base, pushed past its tipping point by two children, with the inquiry's experts stating the top had to move 130–140 mm to topple — a pure rigid-block overturning case with every input published. Wind-driven free-standing-wall cases with published height/thickness/wind are thinner: the Oxgangs gable (69 mph gust, 102.5 mm outer leaf pulled off because wall ties lacked 50 mm embedment) and the West Virginia/Tennessee block wall (FACE 2014-02, two dead, wind gust, dimensions in the full PDF which was not retrievable). The BRE Good Building Guide 14 height tables (via a council leaflet) give the h/t limits (roughly 5–7) used in the UK to judge such walls.

### Cited Findings

#### Case 1.1 — Liberton High School privacy wall, Edinburgh, 1 April 2014 (Fatal Accident Inquiry [2017] FAI 14, Sheriff Principal Mhairi Stephen QC, 23 Aug 2017)
- Structure: a freestanding "privacy wall" in the girls' changing room of the 1959 PE block, "some 3.65ms in length and 2.3ms high", "supported on its bottom edge or foundation but is not connected in any way to any other part of the room" — [FAI determination (PDF, p.8)](https://www.scotcourts.gov.uk/media/of2fhcls/fai-keane-grace-wallis-bennett.pdf)
- Construction: 1955 drawings show thickness 4½ in; "built of unreinforced single brick masonry approximately 4 inches or 105mm thick", terrazzo render both faces with Bakelite strips; finished thickness approximately 140 mm — [FAI determination, para 24](https://www.scotcourts.gov.uk/media/of2fhcls/fai-keane-grace-wallis-bennett.pdf)
- Mass: "The weight of the wall that fell was approximately 1865kg or 1.9 tonnes" — [FAI, para 16](https://www.scotcourts.gov.uk/media/of2fhcls/fai-keane-grace-wallis-bennett.pdf)
- Trigger: two first-year pupils "braced between the privacy wall and the shower wall" (back on the privacy wall, feet on the opposite wall, "chimneying"); the gap between the walls was less than a metre — [FAI, paras 16, 32](https://www.scotcourts.gov.uk/media/of2fhcls/fai-keane-grace-wallis-bennett.pdf)
- Mechanism (reconstructed by BRE and HSE expert Hatfield, accepted by the Sheriff): the wall "separated close to its base and had been cracked" along a horizontal plane originating at the horizontal Bakelite strip near the base; "Accepting that the mortar bond between the bricks had cracked the wall would remain stable by virtue of its own weight unless lateral pressure was applied... The joint expert report indicates that the wall would have to tilt approximately 130 to 140mm at its top edge before it tilted far enough to fall" — [FAI, paras 28–31](https://www.scotcourts.gov.uk/media/of2fhcls/fai-keane-grace-wallis-bennett.pdf)
- Expert's statics reasoning: "If a horizontal force is applied to a wall then the leverage effect (and hence the tensile force) on a mortar bed increases the further that mortar bed is situated below the point of application. Therefore... that crack is more likely to occur near the base of a wall" — [FAI, para 30 quoting Hatfield para 85](https://www.scotcourts.gov.uk/media/of2fhcls/fai-keane-grace-wallis-bennett.pdf)
- Finding: "The immediate cause of the wall collapsing was the application of a lateral force to the wall sufficient to push it beyond its tipping point"; two pupils chimneying "would probably be able to exert sufficient force on the cracked wall"; one pupil reduces that to "a possibility only"; girls merely leaning on it "would not create enough force to cause it to move or tilt" — [FAI, paras (b), 31–32](https://www.scotcourts.gov.uk/media/of2fhcls/fai-keane-grace-wallis-bennett.pdf)
- Outcome: one child killed (crushed on the room side); the wall fell as a unit toward the room side — [FAI, para 16](https://www.scotcourts.gov.uk/media/of2fhcls/fai-keane-grace-wallis-bennett.pdf); at least 13 other schools were later found to have similar walls — [Scottish Construction Now](https://www.scottishconstructionnow.com/articles/inherently-unstable-wall-to-blame-for-keane-wallis-bennet-death-finds-inquiry)

#### Case 1.2 — Oxgangs Primary School gable outer leaf, Edinburgh, 29 January 2016 (Cole Inquiry, Feb 2017)
- Event: "a substantial section of the upper level of the external brickwork face of a non-structural cavity wall to a two-storey gable of the school, weighing approximately 9 tons, collapsed onto the pathway below" during Storm Gertrude, before school opening — [Cole report §5.1 (PDF)](https://www.ciob.org/media/113/download)
- Wall build-up (standard across the 17 PPP1 schools): inner leaf 140 mm blockwork; 120 mm cavity (70 mm insulation + 50 mm air space); outer leaf 102.5 mm facing brick or 100 mm rendered block; wall ties required to have minimum 50 mm embedment in both leaves; head restraints and windposts specified — [Cole report §5.2–5.3](https://www.ciob.org/media/113/download)
- Wind: Met Office Claim Check Service "recorded gusts of up to 69 mph around the time of the collapse of the wall, which were at a level 20% lower than the design requirements as specified in the British Standard BS 6399"; strongest gusts at 07:00; collapse unwitnessed but thought to be around then — [Cole report §5.3.6](https://www.ciob.org/media/113/download). 69 mph = 30.8 m/s (converted).
- Cause: "poor quality construction in the building of the wall which failed to achieve the required minimum embedment of 50mm for the wall ties, particularly in the outer leaf... The limited embedment of the wall ties in the outer leaf meant that the outer face of brick was simply pulled away from the inner leaf of blockwork due to the action of the wind"; "if the wall had been designed and built to the required appropriate standard it would have been able to withstand the level of wind loading" — [Cole report §3.4](https://www.ciob.org/media/113/download)
- Context: ties "variously missing, of the wrong type or poorly fitted"; similar defects later found at 88 Scottish schools — [Public Finance](https://www.publicfinance.co.uk/node/25526); [Construction Index](https://www.theconstructionindex.co.uk/news/view/edinburgh-school-collapse-revealed-to-have-far-reaching-implications)

#### Case 1.3 — Block wall felled by wind gust, FACE 2014-02, 18 April 2013 (two dead)
- "A wind gust caused the block wall to fall onto the laborer, pipefitter, and utility foreman"; laborer caulking the wall's expansion joints, the other two in an adjacent trench; two died at scene — [NIOSH stacks record 167244](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/167244); [Safety+Health case report](https://www.safetyandhealthmagazine.com/?p=23796)
- NIOSH's listed contributing factors (from the search summary of the full report): "inadequate bracing for the block wall and wall height extending too far above the bracing" — [Safety+Health FACEValue](https://safetyandhealthmagazine.com/articles/17415-facevalue-two-workers-crushed-to-death-by-falling-wall); recommendations include following a masonry wall bracing plan and assigning a competent person to monitor wind speeds — [Safety+Health](https://www.safetyandhealthmagazine.com/?p=23796)
- State conflict: NIOSH titles it "...Falling Block Wall—Tennessee" ([stacks](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/167244)); Safety+Health attributes it to the West Virginia FACE Program ([S+H](https://www.safetyandhealthmagazine.com/?p=23796)). Full PDF: https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/167244/cdc_167244_DS1.pdf (19 pp, not retrievable in this session — see Gaps).

#### Case 1.4 — Boundary wall onto public path, UK, August 2016 (HSE investigation, outcome not found)
- A six-year-old severely injured when a "50 years old, single skin construction and about 1.8 metres (6 feet) high" boundary wall collapsed; HSE investigation open at time of writing; LABC: most free-standing walls "often have excessive height to thickness proportions, inadequate piers, buttressing or reinforcing" — [LABC](https://www.labc.co.uk/news/how-safe-your-boundary-wall)

#### Case 1.5 — Confined brick wall collapse under moderate wind, Medellín, Colombia (Aviram, Badillo, Prieto & Jaramillo 2019)
- Citation: *Revista Ingeniería de Construcción* 34(1), 65–80, 2019, DOI 10.4067/S0718-50732019000100065 — [EAFIT repository record](https://repository.eafit.edu.co/items/3c722662-a126-4feb-ab1f-c5967289c7b9); [journal page](https://horizonteenfermeria.uc.cl/index.php/ric/article/view/18417)
- Method: compared models "from a rigid cantilever of the masonry alone to frame models of the surrounding concrete columns and beams, plus a finite element model"; wind from interpolated nearby measurements; outputs are top deflection and demand/capacity ratios; "the recommended parameters and assumptions lead in all cases to the correct determination of the wall's imminent collapse under the estimated wind demands" — [EAFIT](https://repository.eafit.edu.co/items/3c722662-a126-4feb-ab1f-c5967289c7b9). Note this is a confined-masonry (RC-framed) wall, not a free-standing one; PDF unreadable in this session.

#### Guidance used to judge free-standing walls
- BRE Good Building Guide 14 (simple-plan free-standing walls) gives "rule of thumb guidance for stable construction" under wind; GBG 13 is the survey checklist; GBG 19 covers reinforced/diaphragm walls — [IHS/BRE listing](https://cis.ihs.com//CIS/document/83988); [LABC](https://labc.co.uk/news/how-building-regulations-may-affect-garden-walls)
- Maximum heights (mm) by UK wind zone, "based on the guidance given in Good Building Guides 13 & 14": half-brick 100 mm: 525/450/400/375 (zones 1–4); one-brick 215 mm: 1450/1300/1175/1075; one-and-a-half brick 325 mm: 2400/2175/2000/1825; 100 mm block: 450/400/350/325; 200 mm block: 1050/925/850/775; 300 mm block: 2000/1825/1650/1525 — [Milton Keynes Council leaflet](https://www.milton-keynes.gov.uk/sites/default/files/2022-03/garden-walls.pdf)
- Walls under construction: WorkSafe Victoria says brace where "wall height exceeds 10 times the wall thickness", lower if wind likely to exceed 30 km/h — [WorkSafe Vic](https://www.worksafe.vic.gov.au/safety-alerts/danger-freestanding-masonry-walls); same 10t rule citing AS 3700, braces ≤ 2 m apart — [CFMEU guide](https://cfmmeu.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/WALLcollapse_web.pdf)
- Demolition: OSHA 29 CFR 1926.854(b) — "no wall section which is more than one story in height shall be permitted to stand alone without lateral bracing" (as quoted in FACE 94MN020, [stacks 167200](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/167200)); a secondary summary gives a 6 ft (1.8 m) threshold for the same rule — [FindLaw CFR text](https://codes.findlaw.com/cfr/title-29-labor/cfr-sect-29-1926-854/) (verify which wording is current).

### Inferences
- **Liberton test (strongest KQ1 case).** Inputs: block 3.65 m × 2.3 m × 0.14 m (or 0.105 m brick core + render), mass 1865 kg (ρ ≈ 1590 kg/m³ at 140 mm), zero tensile strength at a horizontal joint ~100 mm above the base (the inquiry found it fully cracked), rigid floor. Assertions: (a) the wall stands under self-weight alone; (b) a horizontal top displacement of ≈ t (140 mm; inquiry's experts: 130–140 mm) is the tipping displacement — matches the rigid-block result that the top moves exactly *t* when the centroid passes over the toe; (c) the quasi-static top force to tip is F = W·t/(2h) = 18.3 kN × 0.14/(2×2.3) ≈ 0.56 kN; at a 1.2 m push height ≈ 1.1 kN — consistent with the inquiry's "two pupils probably, one possibly"; (d) a "leaning" load of a few tens of newtons must not move it. Also assert that if the base joint has even modest tensile strength the push required rises sharply — the inquiry's point that the pre-existing crack was necessary.
- **Oxgangs test.** Model the outer leaf as a 102.5 mm free-standing panel (ties removed) of two-storey gable height, pinned at the base only, under a 30.8 m/s gust (q ≈ 0.6 kPa at ρ=1.25 kg/m³; the report's own figure is "20% below BS 6399 design"). Assertion: with ties absent the leaf overturns (p_crit = γ·t²/h ≈ 18 kN/m³ × 0.105² / 6 m ≈ 0.03 kPa ≪ 0.6 kPa); with ties present at embedment the panel stands. The report's conclusion ("would have withstood the wind") is the stand case.
- **BRE table as a sweep.** The GBG14 heights imply permissible h/t of ~5.25 (half-brick, zone 1) down to 3.75 (zone 4), and 6.7 down to 5.0 for one-brick walls. A rigid-block engine with γ ≈ 18–20 kN/m³ and zone wind pressures should find factors of safety against overturning ≳1 at exactly these heights — a tabulated acceptance sweep rather than a single case.

### Gaps
- No HSE research report with recorded wall height/thickness/wind speed for a wind-blown free-standing wall was found; HSE output on this topic is prosecution summaries without geometry.
- FACE 2014-02 wall height, length, thickness, bracing spacing and recorded gust are in the 19-page PDF, which both cdc.gov and stacks.cdc.gov refused to serve (HTML challenge page). The S+H summaries do not carry the numbers.
- The 2016 LABC boundary-wall case: no HSE outcome or wall thickness found.
- GBG14 was read only via a council leaflet; the BRE text and wind-zone map were not accessed.
- Medellín paper full text not retrieved (both the EAFIT bitstream and the journal PDF served HTML).

## KQ2. Demolition engineering cases: supports removed and the resulting collapse (or not) documented — chimney felling, load-bearing wall removal, excavation undermining

### Takeaway
The single best-documented chimney felling is Reid's 1899 account of the 81 m Wallsend stack: cut 0.9 m above ground on the fall side through 61% of the circumference, replaced by tarred timber blocks, fired, fell "exactly on the line marked out" six minutes after lighting — every dimension given. For accidental removals, the FACE Virginia 1998 chimney (7.9–9.1 m, fell "as a single mass" onto an excavator when the adjoining wall was being taken down), the Minnesota 1994 parapet (1.2 m parapet left free-standing >1 storey when the roof came off, fell inward) and the Philadelphia 2013 Market Street wall (lateral support removed over three days; the whole section above the one-storey neighbour fell as one unit) are the clearest. UK HSE prosecutions (Windsor 2020, Bushey 2021, Brighton 2015, Sheffield) document gable and party-wall collapses after roofs, floors, steels or basements were removed, but without dimensions.

### Cited Findings

#### Case 2.1 — Felling of the Wallsend chimney, 14 January 1899 (Frank Reid, Trans. Inst. Mining Engineers vol. XVII, 1898–99)
- Geometry: built 1879; 266 ft high; ground-level outside diameter 21 ft, inside 14 ft; top outside 14 ft, inside 12½ ft; brickwork 3½ ft thick at the bottom and 9 in at the top — [Durham Mining Museum transcription](http://www.dmm.org.uk/transime/u17c-01.htm). Converted: H = 81.1 m; base OD 6.40 m, ID 4.27 m (t = 1.07 m); top OD 4.27 m, ID 3.81 m (t = 0.23 m).
- Method: from 9 Jan 1899 workmen cut out the walls "about 3 feet from the ground" on both sides, working toward the fall direction, replacing brickwork with wooden blocks 18 in high × 15 in wide × 12 in long (457 × 381 × 305 mm) wedged with hardwood; blocks were three 3-in deal boards spaced by 7×3×12 in pieces so tar and sawdust could fill the gaps; "About 40 feet was cut out, and the chimney being at this point 66 feet in circumference, about 26 feet was left at the back" — [DMM](http://www.dmm.org.uk/transime/u17c-01.htm). Converted: cut 12.2 m of a 20.1 m circumference (61%), 7.9 m (39%) of uncut hinge at the back; cut height 0.91 m.
- Fall: blocks soaked in tar and paraffin, fire built round the cut with more fuel on the fall side; paraffin thrown on for a minute; "6 minutes after the lighting of the fire, the chimney fell exactly on the line marked out for on the ground" (nearly due south); cost half that of top-down dismantling and "many thousands of the bricks were recovered" — [DMM](http://www.dmm.org.uk/transime/u17c-01.htm)

#### Case 2.2 — Fred Dibnah's method (secondary accounts, no engineering record)
- Removed bricks from the base, shored with timber props, and once enough was removed that the stack "could no longer hold its own weight" set fire to the props — [Wikipedia: Chimney felling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chimney_felling); staged drilling and packing "on the side which the chimney was to fall", ~90 chimneys felled without explosives — [Heritage Portal](https://www.theheritageportal.co.za/article/south-africa-needs-fred-dibnah); at Canvey Island (1997, concrete stack) a slot was cut and propped with telegraph poles over three weeks, and the chimney "prematurely fell down before the planned demolition" — [Canvey Island archive](https://www.canveyisland.org/cms/history-2/old/the_canvey_island_chimney_disaster)

#### Mid-air fracture of a toppling chimney (theory, not a case)
- Varieschi & Kamiya (arXiv physics/0210033, *Am. J. Phys.* 2003): ruptures during the fall "can be caused by either shear forces, typically developing near the base, or by the bending of the structure... In the latter case the breaking is more likely to occur between one third and one half of the height"; shear is zero at H/3 and has a relative maximum at 2H/3; an earlier literature claim that the break is at the centre of percussion (2H/3) was wrong — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/physics/0210033)
- Chinese blasting-demolition FE work found stress concentrations at about 1/3, 1/2 and 2/3 height of brick chimneys during toppling (search summary only) — [sinoxiv record](https://sinoxiv.napstic.cn/article/5482498)

#### Case 2.3 — Brick chimney falls on excavator during house demolition, Virginia, 2 July 1998 (NIOSH FACE 98-18)
- A 2½-storey stone house ~75% demolished, "leaving a large brick chimney between 26 and 30 feet high and part of a stone wall"; as the operator worked on the remaining wall, a co-worker "saw the chimney sway and fall as a single mass, landing directly across the operator's cab"; operator killed; NIOSH recommended an engineering survey by a competent person before demolition — [NIOSH stacks 167389](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/167389). Converted: 7.9–9.1 m.

#### Case 2.4 — Parapet collapses when roof removed, Minnesota, 6 April 1994 (MN FACE 94MN020)
- Two-storey fire-damaged commercial building; roof being cut into ~25 ft (7.6 m) square sections and craned off; "The two-story building had a brick parapet wall which extended approximately four feet above the roof. Removal of the roof resulted in a free standing brick wall which was more than one story in height"; as the second-to-last roof section was lifted "the brick parapet wall collapsed inward and fell on the victim" standing at a second-floor window; simultaneously the last roof section broke free and the wall it was on collapsed onto the second floor; recommendation: brace free-standing walls over one storey per 29 CFR 1926.854(b) — [FACE 94MN020 (stacks 167200)](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/167200), text also at the archived [CDC page](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/face/stateface/mn/94mn020.html)

#### Case 2.5 — 2136–38 Market Street, Philadelphia, 5 June 2013 (6 dead, 14 injured)
- Four-storey unreinforced masonry building under mechanical demolition fell onto the attached one-storey Salvation Army thrift store; "the entire wall section above the height of the thrift store collapsed in a single unit" — [Penn State failure case page](https://www.engr.psu.edu/ae/thesis/failures/MKP/failures/failures.wikispaces.com/22nd_and_Market_Street_Salvation_Army_Building.html)
- OSHA: "During the three days prior to the collapse, Campbell Construction removed critical, structural supports for the wall that collapsed", violating the rule against removing lateral support from walls more than one storey high; also failed to demolish top-down and to obtain an engineering survey; penalties $397,000 — [US DOL/OSHA release](https://www.dol.gov/newsroom/releases/osha/osha20131114)
- Press-reported unbraced section height ≈ 30 ft (9.1 m) (AP, via [WHYY](https://whyy.org/articles/400000-fines-proposed-in-fatal-philly-building-collapse-officials-say-was-avoidable)); contractor later indicted for third-degree murder — [EHS Today](https://www.ehstoday.com/construction/article/21916014/contractor-griffin-campbell-charged-with-thirddegree-murder-for-philadelphia-building-collapse)

#### Case 2.6 — Gable falls into house after roof removed, Springfield Road, Windsor, 6 August 2020 (HSE prosecution 2025)
- During a loft conversion the builder had workers "remove key supporting timbers and steels" while a chimney breast removed from the first-floor bathroom was still unpropped; "the brick gable wall fell into the building after the roof was removed", seriously injuring three and destroying the first floor; HSE: failed to ensure the structure did not collapse "while it was in a state of temporary weakness"; 13-month sentence suspended — [Construction Index](https://www.theconstructionindex.co.uk/news/view/suspended-prison-sentence-for-builder-who-brought-the-house-down); [HSE press release](https://press.hse.gov.uk/2025/09/29/builder-sentenced-after-house-collapse-injures-three-workers/)

#### Case 2.7 — Gable falls onto neighbour, Grange Road, Bushey, 5 April 2021
- House "reduced to a shell. Its roof, internal walls, and structural support members for both gable ends had been taken out"; no temporary supports; an unsupported gable fell onto the neighbouring property, fracturing a resident's sternum and collarbone; HSE: preventable "by effective planning of the dismantling sequence"; £40,000 fine (St Albans Magistrates, 6 July 2022) — [HSM](https://www.hsmsearch.com/?p=17479)

#### Case 2.8 — Basement excavation undermines a bungaroosh gable, Brighton, February 2015
- Contractor "dug out the basement of the house... undermining the structural integrity of the house"; cracks appeared, engineer's advice ignored; "The gable wall partially collapsed, and the ground floor fell into the basement"; walls were bungaroosh (rubble, timber, flint in lime mortar between shuttering); five months' imprisonment (Hove Crown Court, 2018) — [Construction Index](https://www.theconstructionindex.co.uk/news/view/builder-jailed-over-basement-excavation-collapse)

#### Case 2.9 — Basement lowering undermines central wall, Brook Hill, Sheffield (three-storey building, three injured)
- Work "involved lowering the basement floor and removing several internal walls. While that was underway, the central wall was undermined and collapsed. The floors of the flats above and the front wall then caved in"; no controls to support the building; 12-month custodial sentences and fines; building demolished — search summary of [Construction Index](https://www.theconstructionindex.co.uk/news/view/brothers-banged-up-after-bodged-basement-job-brings-the-house-down) (page itself returned 404 when fetched)

### Inferences
- **Wallsend felling test (best KQ2 case).** Inputs: a tapered hollow brick frustum, H = 81.1 m, base OD 6.40 m / t 1.07 m, top OD 4.27 m / t 0.23 m, γ ≈ 18 kN/m³, built of courses with zero (or small, ~0.1 MPa) joint tension; at z = 0.91 m remove 61% of the ring on the south side (the full 12.2 m arc) leaving a 7.9 m arc at the north. Assertions: (a) with props present the stack stands; (b) with props removed the resultant of self-weight falls outside the residual 39% arc's footprint, the stack rotates about the uncut hinge toward the south, and the fall direction lies within a few degrees of the cut's axis of symmetry ("exactly on the line marked out"); (c) if the fracture criterion is enabled, the shaft should break during the fall somewhere in 0.33–0.5 H (Varieschi & Kamiya), never at the base-only. A useful sub-test: reduce the cut arc until the stack stands — the engine's threshold arc should be close to where the residual-ring kern just contains the weight resultant.
- **Virginia chimney (2.3).** Inputs: free-standing brick stack ~8.5 m tall, plan dimensions unknown (assume ~1 × 1.5 m domestic chimney), initially sharing a face with a stone wall; event = remove the wall / apply an excavator contact load at low level. Assertion: the chimney overturns as one rigid body ("fell as a single mass"), not course-by-course, which is what a mortar with modest tension predicts; a cohesionless-stack model would instead crumble — this case discriminates between the two.
- **Minnesota parapet (2.4).** Inputs: a 1.2 m parapet on top of a two-storey wall (~7 m) made free-standing by removing the roof diaphragm; disturbance from the crane lift. Assertion: the parapet-plus-wall assembly becomes a cantilever restrained only at ground; the parapet falls *inward* (toward the removed roof) — the fall direction is the thing to assert, since the inward side had just lost its restraint and the lift applied an inward pull.
- **Philadelphia (2.5).** Inputs: a ~9 m tall (press figure) unreinforced brick wall left standing above a one-storey neighbour after floor joists (lateral restraint) were removed; disturbance from an excavator. Assertion: collapse of the whole unrestrained section "in a single unit" onto the lower roof once a small lateral push is applied; with floors modelled as restraints the wall stands.
- **UK alteration cases (2.6–2.9)** are mechanism-rich but geometry-poor. They support assertions of *which element goes first* (gable after roof removed; floors after central wall undermined; ground floor into basement after gable base undermined) rather than numeric thresholds.

### Gaps
- No engineering case study of a *notched* brick chimney that fell the wrong way or stood was found; Reid 1899 is a success case only. Dibnah's method has no published dimensions.
- FACE 98-18's full report (chimney plan dimensions, cut/contact details) was not retrievable (HTML challenge page).
- Philadelphia: no official wall height/thickness; the 30 ft figure is AP reporting; the OSHA citation documents themselves were not located.
- No HSE case gives floor loads or spans for a load-bearing wall removal; "with the floor loads and spans" could not be satisfied from HSE material.

## KQ3. Progressive collapse of panel/block structures: Ronan Point 1968 and later cases; full-scale removal tests on masonry buildings

### Takeaway
Ronan Point is well quantified in secondary literature: a 22-storey Larsen-Nielsen panel tower; a flat-90 (18th floor) gas explosion of less than 10 psi (69 kPa) blew out a flank wall that tests showed could be displaced at ~3 psi (21 kPa); the south-east corner living-room bays of floors 19–22 fell, then floor 18 was overloaded and the collapse propagated to the podium; the inquiry (Griffiths, Pugsley & Saunders 1968) found no alternative load path and that wind or fire could have done the same; demolition in 1986 found joints stuffed with rubbish and untightened tie-plate nuts. No full-scale test that removed a load-bearing wall from a masonry building was found; the Ohio State full-scale series removed *columns* from masonry, RC and steel buildings and its masonry report could not be accessed.

### Cited Findings

#### Case 3.1 — Ronan Point, Canning Town, 16 May 1968
- Building: 22 storeys of flats on a cast-in-place podium; 210 ft (64 m) high, 80 × 60 ft (24.4 × 18.3 m) footprint; Larsen-Nielsen precast panel system "originally intended for buildings of no more than six storeys" — [Penn State failure page](https://www.engr.psu.edu/ae/thesis/failures/MKP/failures/failures.wikispaces.com/Ronan_Point.html); "22 stories of apartments built using precast concrete panels which rested on a cast-in-place podium" — [NBSIR 76-1106](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/IR/nbsir76-1106.pdf)
- Trigger: gas explosion in flat 90, 18th floor, 05:45; explosion pressure "less than 10 lb/in²" (<69 kPa) inferred from the tenant's undamaged hearing (Delatte 2009) — [Penn State](https://www.engr.psu.edu/ae/thesis/failures/MKP/failures/failures.wikispaces.com/Ronan_Point.html)
- Capacity of the walls: "The pressure that could displace the exterior walls: 3 lb/in² [21 kPa]" (Levy & Salvadori 1992) — [Penn State](https://www.engr.psu.edu/ae/thesis/failures/MKP/failures/failures.wikispaces.com/Ronan_Point.html); other secondary accounts give 19.3 kPa (2.8 psi) from BRS/Imperial College tests, 1.7 kPa (0.25 psi) for the internal kitchen/living-room partitions and 21 kPa (3 psi) for the exterior wall (Griffiths et al. 1968 via Rouse & Delatte 2003) — search summary of [UNC Charlotte case study](https://engineering.charlotte.edu/asce-failure-case-studies/?p=168) (page could not be fetched); Pugsley's full-scale test in August 1968 showed the H2 joint failed at 1.8 psi (12.4 kPa) and the National Building Agency had calculated 1.4 psi (9.7 kPa) — [Building.co.uk archive](https://www.building.co.uk/comment/back-issues/3053297.article)
- Sequence: "Phase 1: southeast corner walls on floors 19 to 22 collapsed. Phase 2: floor 18 was overloaded and collapsed onto floor 17, progressing to ground level"; 4 dead, 17 injured — [Penn State](https://www.engr.psu.edu/ae/thesis/failures/MKP/failures/failures.wikispaces.com/Ronan_Point.html); NBS: "loss of support caused a chain reaction of collapse upwards to the roof. The collapse also progressed almost to the ground as debris from above fell on successive floors below" — [NBSIR 76-1106 §1.1](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/IR/nbsir76-1106.pdf)
- Why the flank wall went: the blast "lifted upward a portion of the ceiling... causing loss of the gravity load between the floor slab and flank wall necessary to keep the flank wall anchored" — the joint relied on friction from gravity — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronan_Point); Pearson & Delatte: the connections "relied, in large part, on friction" and the tower "lacked alternate load paths" — [ASCE JPCF 19(2) 172–177 abstract](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%290887-3828%282005%2919%3A2%28172%29)
- Inquiry (Griffiths, Pugsley, Saunders, HMSO 1968): design complied with regulations but the building was "not an acceptable building"; focus on the "lack of load redistribution capability, or what it termed 'alternate paths'" — [NBSIR 76-1106](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/IR/nbsir76-1106.pdf); design wind 63 mph (28 m/s) versus up to 105 mph (47 m/s) at 200 ft; "Strong winds or fire could also have caused progressive collapse" — [Penn State](https://www.engr.psu.edu/ae/thesis/failures/MKP/failures/failures.wikispaces.com/Ronan_Point.html)
- Regulatory consequence: Circular 62/68 required either an alternate load path on loss of a single critical member or local resistance to 5 psi (34 kN/m²) — [NBSIR 76-1106](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/IR/nbsir76-1106.pdf); existing blocks 17 kPa (2.5 psi) if gas removed — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronan_Point)
- 1986 demolition: joints "full of voids and rubbish", steel tie-plate nuts not tightened — [Penn State](https://www.engr.psu.edu/ae/thesis/failures/MKP/failures/failures.wikispaces.com/Ronan_Point.html); "Holes that should have been filled with concrete were stuffed with rubbish and newspaper" — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronan_Point)
- Counter-example in the same report: a panel building in Algeria "lost several panels due to a bomb explosion but did not collapse" because the remaining elements had strength and continuity — [NBSIR 76-1106 §1.1 and fig. 3](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/IR/nbsir76-1106.pdf)

#### Case 3.2 — Emilia 2012 precast industrial buildings (friction seats): the clearest modern "block slides off its support" case
- Mechanism: "the main recorded damage is either the loss of support of structural horizontal elements, due to the failure of friction beam-to-column and roof-to-beam connections, or the collapse of the cladding panels" — [Magliulo et al. 2014, Earthquake Spectra 30(2) 891–912 (accepted MS)](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1474491/1/2014%20Magliulo%20et%20al_Earth%20Spectra_Emilia%20eqk.pdf)
- Ground motion: station MRN (Mirandola, soil C) 20 May 2012 — PGA 0.264 g N-S, 0.261 g E-W, spectral ordinates up to 1 g; code PGA for Mirandola (10%/50 yr) 0.140 g; region outside seismic zones until 2003 — [Magliulo et al. 2014](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1474491/1/2014%20Magliulo%20et%20al_Earth%20Spectra_Emilia%20eqk.pdf)
- Friction: neoprene-to-concrete coefficient "varies in the range 0.09 – 0.13 for compressive stress varying between 1.7 MPa and 5.3 MPa" (Magliulo et al. 2011); "the loss of support mechanism is immediately checked comparing the friction coefficient with the acceleration spectral ordinates in g"; safety factor SF = μ/(Sa/g) "is much below 1 for a wide range of periods"; even μ = 0.2–0.4 (Caltrans) would not have avoided loss of support — [Magliulo et al. 2014](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1474491/1/2014%20Magliulo%20et%20al_Earth%20Spectra_Emilia%20eqk.pdf)
- Seat geometry rule (DM 3/12/1987): beam end support "must be not smaller than 8 cm + l/300, where l is the clear beam span in centimeters"; the 1965 circular's T/N ≤ 0.35 implicitly assumed μ = 0.35 — [Ercolino et al. 2014](https://gala.gre.ac.uk/id/eprint/14263/1/14263_ERCOLINO_Emilia_Earthquake_2014.pdf)
- Casualties and stock: the 29 May Mw 5.8 event caused a further 20 deaths; precast share of buildings near Mirandola up to 9% — [Ercolino et al. 2014](https://gala.gre.ac.uk/id/eprint/14263/1/14263_ERCOLINO_Emilia_Earthquake_2014.pdf)

#### Full-scale removal tests
- Ohio State series: "single or multiple first-story columns physically removed" from masonry, RC and steel buildings; the authors state full-scale experimental evidence "is either very limited or does not exist" — search summary of [OSU Knowledge Bank item](https://kb.osu.edu/items/bc4509cc-c31d-5864-866c-cd5cc6c856c3) (fetch returned 403)
- Closest masonry-wall analogue: a purpose-built two-storey RC frame with masonry infill tested after corner-column removal with 38 strain gauges, 38 LVDTs and 2 accelerometers — [UPV record](https://riunet.upv.es/entities/publication/7344f596-d8fb-4f83-88d7-651036c4cd85)

### Inferences
- **Ronan Point test.** Inputs: a stack of 22 storeys of precast flank-wall panels and floor slabs at the south-east corner; wall-to-slab joints with friction only (μ·N) and essentially zero tension; a uniform internal pressure on the 18th-floor flank panel. Assertions: (a) the flank panel is pushed out at 12–21 kPa (the H2 joint test 12.4 kPa; wall displacement 19–21 kPa), far below the <69 kPa blast; (b) once the panel is gone, the four storeys above lose bearing and fall (floors 19–22 first); (c) the debris of four floors (impact load) exceeds the capacity of floor 18 and the collapse propagates to the podium; (d) the collapse stays confined to the one bay (the rest of the tower stood). A second, "stands", variant: give the slab–wall joint continuity ties with modest tensile capacity and the engine should bridge the lost panel (the Algeria counter-example and the post-1968 alternate-path rule).
- **Emilia test (recommended, because the inputs are all published).** Inputs: a beam of weight W on a neoprene seat with μ = 0.09–0.13 (or 0.35 for the code assumption), seat length 8 cm + l/300, horizontal base acceleration with Sa/g up to 1.0 (MRN record), vertical component optional. Assertions: sliding starts when Sa/g > μ; cumulative slip over the record exceeds the seat length → loss of support; with a dowel (shear capacity) or μ ≥ Sa/g the beam stays. The beauty of this case is that the investigators themselves reduced it to a rigid-block friction check (their eq. 2).

### Gaps
- The Griffiths/Pugsley/Saunders report itself (HMSO 1968) and Pearson & Delatte 2005 full text were not accessible; panel thicknesses and the H2 joint geometry are not in the open sources used. The various pressure numbers (0.25 / 1.4 / 1.8 / 2.8 / 3 psi) refer to different elements and tests and should not be merged.
- No full-scale test removing a load-bearing *masonry wall* with measured response was found; the OSU masonry column-removal report was not readable.
- Emilia: no single collapsed building with its own seat length and beam weight was retrieved (the Ercolino/Magliulo Mirandola case-study building paper was not fetched).

## KQ4. Earthquake out-of-plane overturning of URM façades, gables and parapets (L'Aquila 2009, Christchurch 2010–11, Emilia 2012) with PGA and geometry

### Takeaway
Christchurch is the quantified dataset: Giaretton, Dizhur, da Porto & Ingham (Earthquake Spectra 2016) catalogued 959 URM parapets (typical thickness 230 mm, 48% between 0.5 and 1.0 m tall) and binned them by neighbourhood PGA; in the 22 February 2011 event (0.7 g in the CBD, 1.0 g at Lyttelton) 371 collapsed versus 226 standing in the 0.51–0.75 g bin, while in September 2010 (mostly ≤0.5 g) only 13% collapsed. That is directly comparable with the rigid-block threshold a/g = t/h ≈ 0.23–0.46 for those parapets. L'Aquila gives PGA (0.35–0.66 g within 10 km) and wall thickness (300–500 mm stone or brick) but mechanism descriptions are qualitative; repeatedly, iron ties were what prevented global overturning.

### Cited Findings

#### Christchurch 2010–2011
- Inventory: 959 URM parapets existed before 2010, "with 60% (580) of them having collapsed during the 2010/2011 Canterbury earthquake sequence"; 63% (604) in the CBD; 58% (557) on two-storey buildings; 48% (465) were 500–1000 mm high, 38% < 500 mm, 13% > 1000 mm; "a typical thickness equal to 230 mm (two-leaf-thick wall)"; a parapet was counted only if height ≥ thickness — [Giaretton et al. 2016, Earthquake Spectra 32(4) 2377–2397, DOI 10.1193/121715EQS184M (PDF)](https://research.unipd.it/bitstream/11577/3213625/1/Giaretton%20Dizhur%20daPorto%20Ingham%202016%20Post-Earthquake%20Reconnaissance%20of%20Unreinforced%20and%20Retrofitted%20Masonry%20Parapets.pdf)
- PGA assignment: neighbourhood horizontal PGA from GeoNet records; 22 Feb 2011 "PGA was high, with 0.7 g registered in the Christchurch CBD and 1.0 g registered in Lyttelton" — [Giaretton et al. 2016](https://research.unipd.it/bitstream/11577/3213625/1/Giaretton%20Dizhur%20daPorto%20Ingham%202016%20Post-Earthquake%20Reconnaissance%20of%20Unreinforced%20and%20Retrofitted%20Masonry%20Parapets.pdf)
- Table 2 (standing / collapsed, numbers of parapets): 4 Sep 2010 (438 parapets): 0.20–0.25 g 104/15; 0.26–0.50 g 276/43; total 87% standing, 13% collapsed. 22 Feb 2011 (881 parapets): 0.20–0.25 g 39/26; 0.26–0.50 g 95/83; 0.51–0.75 g 226/371; 0.76–1.00 g 18/23; 43% standing, 57% collapsed. 13 June 2011 (96 parapets): 0.20–0.25 g 12/12; 0.26–0.50 g 18/48; 0.51–0.75 g 0/2; 0.76–1.00 g 4/0 — [Giaretton et al. 2016, Table 2](https://research.unipd.it/bitstream/11577/3213625/1/Giaretton%20Dizhur%20daPorto%20Ingham%202016%20Post-Earthquake%20Reconnaissance%20of%20Unreinforced%20and%20Retrofitted%20Masonry%20Parapets.pdf)
- Failure categories after Feb 2011, two-storey buildings: 27% (153) collapsed together with out-of-plane failure of the wall below; 18% (99) "overturning failure of the parapet"; 15% (84) partial collapse; of those standing, 9% (48) showed horizontal cracking "indicated... initiation of overturning"; as-built 40% standing / 60% collapsed versus retrofitted 52% / 48% — [Giaretton et al. 2016](https://research.unipd.it/bitstream/11577/3213625/1/Giaretton%20Dizhur%20daPorto%20Ingham%202016%20Post-Earthquake%20Reconnaissance%20of%20Unreinforced%20and%20Retrofitted%20Masonry%20Parapets.pdf)
- Deaths: 42 people died from URM failures; "at least three failed buildings collapsed onto neighbouring buildings" — [Royal Commission Interim Report §3.3](https://canterbury.royalcommission.govt.nz/Interim-Report-Section-3.3); hearing transcript: of 41 URM deaths, 36 "were as a result of the façade or the walls of these buildings collapsing outwards" — [Royal Commission transcript 12 Dec 2011](https://canterbury.royalcommission.govt.nz/documents-by-key/20111214.1733/$file/TRANS.20111212.pdf); 70% (35) of URM-façade deaths were outside the buildings — [Giaretton et al. 2016](https://research.unipd.it/bitstream/11577/3213625/1/Giaretton%20Dizhur%20daPorto%20Ingham%202016%20Post-Earthquake%20Reconnaissance%20of%20Unreinforced%20and%20Retrofitted%20Masonry%20Parapets.pdf)
- Mechanisms (Ingham & Griffith for the Royal Commission, Sept 2010 event): "unsupported and unreinforced brick chimneys performed poorly"; parapets failed along frontages and side walls and landed on awnings whose tie rods pulled out of the masonry by punching shear; out-of-plane wall damage "indicated poor or no anchorage to the timber diaphragm"; walls carried little vertical load beyond self-weight and roofs were mostly undamaged — [Royal Commission Interim Report §3.3](https://canterbury.royalcommission.govt.nz/Interim-Report-Section-3.3)
- Church gables: out-of-plane gable failures at the Chinese Methodist Church, Merivale and Knox Presbyterian; a bell tower that "collapsed away from the main church building"; St Peter's: "the peaks of transept parapets fell (NS direction) and peaks of quire parapets fell (EW direction) in subsequent" events — [Anagnostopoulou, Bruneau & Gavin, NZSEE Bulletin 43(4) 2010](https://www.eng.buffalo.edu/~bruneau/NZSEE%202010%20Anagnostopoulou%20Bruneau%20Gavin.pdf)

#### L'Aquila 6 April 2009 (Mw 6.3)
- Ground motion: stations within 10 km recorded PGA > 0.35 g; AQV (Centro Valle, 4.9 km) 0.66 g; AQA 0.44 g — [Oyarzo-Vera & Griffith, NZSEE Bulletin 42(4) 2009, p.302 ff.](https://bulletin.nzsee.org.nz/article/view/293/279)
- Construction: "(a) stone masonry walls, 300 – 500 mm thick, which were often rubble filled... (b) clay masonry walls, 300 – 500 mm thick, with timber" floors/roofs; "The most common failure modes observed were associated with out-of-plane failures of parapets, gable-ends and in some cases entire walls"; damage concentrated at diaphragm level; RC ring beams added at roof level were seen on collapsed walls in Onna ("Collapse of walls with R/C ring beam at roof level"); out-of-plane failures photographed at San Gregorio and Onna; 305 killed (203 in L'Aquila, 37 in Onna) — [Oyarzo-Vera & Griffith 2009](https://bulletin.nzsee.org.nz/article/view/293/279)
- Ties: in the historic centre, "flexural mechanism of facade wall... iron ties avoided global overturning"; "collapse mechanism of the upper zone of the wall... roof structure not well anchored to the wall. Iron ties avoided global overturning"; "collapse of the central zone of a wall determined by the horizontal action of the wooden beam" — [Calderoni et al. 2009, ReLUIS preliminary report (photo captions)](https://www.reluis.it/doc/pdf/Aquila/Report_masonry_building_1.pdf)
- Deficiencies: "unconfined gable walls, excessive wall thicknesses without connection with each other" among the common faults of poorly built masonry — [Kaplan et al., NHESS 10, 499–507, 2010](https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/10/499/2010/)
- d'Ayala & Paganoni, "Assessment and analysis of damage in L'Aquila historic city centre after 6th April 2009", *Bull. Earthquake Eng.* 9(1) 81–104, DOI 10.1007/s10518-010-9224-4 — the standard mechanism-by-mechanism survey; citation only, abstract not accessible (Springer and Structurae returned 403) — [Structurae record](https://structurae.info/fr/litterature/article-de-revue/assessment-and-analysis-of-damage-in-l-aquila-historic-city-centre-after-6th-april-2009)

#### Emilia 2012
- Masonry damage papers were not retrieved; the Emilia precast loss-of-support case is catalogued under KQ3 (Case 3.2), where the recorded PGA (0.26 g) and friction coefficients are given.

### Inferences
- **Christchurch parapet fragility test (best KQ4 case).** Inputs: a free-standing parapet 230 mm thick, heights 0.5–1.0 m (so t/h = 0.23–0.46), γ ≈ 18 kN/m³, on a rigid roof-line base; horizontal base acceleration pulses or records scaled to the Table 2 bins. Assertions: (a) under quasi-static tilt the parapet overturns at a/g = t/h (≈ 0.23–0.46 g); (b) under records with PGA 0.20–0.25 g the great majority stand (Sept 2010: 104 standing vs 15 collapsed) — the engine should show rocking initiation but no overturning for h ≤ 1 m; (c) under PGA 0.51–0.75 g a majority overturn (371 of 597); (d) a parapet braced back to the roof structure (steel brace, retrofit) stands. Because real rocking blocks survive accelerations well above t/h for short pulses (Housner), the test should be stated as "collapse fraction rises steeply between 0.25 g and 0.5 g", not as a single sharp threshold.
- **L'Aquila façade test.** Inputs: a two-storey façade 300–500 mm thick, ~6–7 m tall (t/h ≈ 0.05–0.08), free of ties, floors as non-restraining timber, PGA 0.35–0.66 g. Assertions: without ties the façade overturns about its base (or about the first-floor line, the "upper zone" mechanism when the roof is unanchored); with iron ties at floor levels the global overturning is prevented and only local mechanisms remain. The ReLUIS captions give exactly these stand/fall pairs.

### Gaps
- No L'Aquila source gave a *specific* wall with height, thickness, restraint and recorded PGA together; the numbers above are population-level.
- Christchurch: Giaretton's dataset is by neighbourhood PGA bin, not per-parapet record; the retrofitted parapets' failures were often because the façade below failed, so they are not clean overturning cases.
- Emilia 2012 masonry (farmhouses, churches) out-of-plane data not retrieved.
- Royal Commission Volume 4 and the Ingham/Griffith February-2011 report PDFs were not readable (HTML challenge page).

## KQ5. Multi-drum stone columns and classical temples: thresholds and documented survivals

### Takeaway
The quantitative anchor is Psycharis, Fragiadakis & Stefanou (EESD 2013): a Parthenon Pronaos column 10.08 m tall (shaft 9.38 m, 12 drums, 1.65 m base → 1.28 m top), analysed in 3DEC with Coulomb friction, used to build fragility curves; its monolithic rigid-block tilt threshold is atan(1.65/10.08) ≈ 9.3°, i.e. a static a/g ≈ 0.16. Konstantinidis & Makris (2005) found drums slide even when a/g < μ, that free drums respond more controllably than a monolith, and that typical columns survive near-fault records of Mw 6.0–7.4; Drosos & Anastasopoulos (EESD 2014) shake-table marble models "hardly suffered permanent deformation" under the strongest Greek records, with collapse only under harsh directivity pulses, and PGA a poor predictor. No documented collapse of a real colonnade with measured input was found.

### Cited Findings
- Column geometry (used as a typical example, not to assess the Parthenon): "total height of 10.08 m, being composed of a shaft of 9.38 m and a capital... diameters ranging from 1.65 m at the base to 1.28 m at the top. The shaft was divided into 12 drums of different height according to actual measurements of the columns of the Pronaos"; modelled in 3DEC with Coulomb friction, joint normal and shear stiffness 1 GPa/m, no numerical damping during rocking, no connectors between drums ("the only connectors present... were used... during the erection... and not to provide a shear resistant mechanism") — [Psycharis, Fragiadakis & Stefanou 2013, EESD 42(14) 2061–2079 (arXiv 1312.4484)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.4484)
- Response character: governed by "the sliding, the rocking and the wobbling of the individual, practically rigid, stone drums, which translate and rotate independently or in groups"; the Olympieion of Athens columns (one with 14 drums) used as illustration; columns show "decreased vulnerability for very strong earthquakes of magnitude 7 or larger" — [Psycharis et al. 2013](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.4484)
- Konstantinidis & Makris: "Drums slide relative to each other even when the ground acceleration's g-value is below the interface friction coefficient"; "Typical multidrum columns can survive strong ground motions recorded near causative faults for earthquakes of Mw 6.0 to 7.4"; free-to-dislocate columns "respond more controllably than monolithic columns of the same size and slenderness"; wooden poles' shear strength has only a marginal effect; "stiff metallic shear links in-between column drums may have an undesirable role" — [WIT Trans. Built Env. 81, 2005, DOI 10.2495/ERES050121](http://www.witpress.com/elibrary/wit-transactions-on-the-built-environment/81/15050) (abbreviated version of EESD 34, 2005)
- Shake-table: reduced-scale marble column and portal at NTUA, Ricker pulses and real records; single columns "hardly deformed permanently under the strongest motions recorded in Greece. Collapse was probable only for extremely harsh, directivity-affected motions"; portals more robust; "Unlike PGA, maximum spectral displacement and the length scale L were effective intensity measures" — [Drosos & Anastasopoulos 2014, EESD 43(11) 1703–1723, DOI 10.1002/eqe.2418](https://discovery.dundee.ac.uk/en/publications/shaking-table-testing-of-multidrum-columns-and-portals/)
- Other small-scale tests: Buzov, Radnić, Grgić & Baloević increased base acceleration to failure and found "increasing the number of blocks can significantly increase or decrease ultimate bearing capacity, depending on the accelerogram used" — [CROSBI record](https://www.bib.irb.hr/945596); a single-epistyle study: "for low frequency ground excitations, the exhibited response is dominated by rocking, while sliding prevails... at very high predominant frequencies", and overturning acceleration increases with frequency — [UNIC record](https://pure.unic.ac.cy/en/publications/earthquake-vulnerability-of-ancient-multi-drum-columns-with-a-sin/)

### Inferences
- **Static tilt test.** Inputs: the Psycharis column (12 drums, 1.65 → 1.28 m, 10.08 m), density of Pentelic marble ≈ 2700 kg/m³, Coulomb friction between drums (μ ≈ 0.6–0.7 marble-on-marble is the usual assumption; the paper's table value was not legible). Assertions: on a slowly tilting base the column stands up to atan(B/H) ≈ 9.3° if drums do not slide, and the first joint to open is the base (largest overturning moment about the smallest lever? — no: the base has the largest diameter; the engine should open the joint whose t/h-to-top ratio is smallest; for a tapered shaft that is near the base) — the point of the test is that the engine's chosen hinge joint should match the DEM result, which is a reproducible number once the model is built.
- **Pulse tests.** Assertions, from the three papers: (a) a half-sine/Ricker pulse with amplitude < 0.16 g produces no uplift; (b) pulses between 0.16 g and several multiples of it produce rocking without overturning when the pulse period is short relative to the column's size (Housner size effect); (c) drum sliding appears before overturning for high-frequency input even at a/g < μ (K&M); (d) a monolithic column of the same envelope overturns at a *lower* pulse amplitude than the jointed one for long-period pulses (K&M "more controllably"). These are mechanism assertions, not thresholds — the papers deliberately say PGA is a poor intensity measure.
- Survival of the Parthenon/Olympieion colonnades through 2,500 years of Greek seismicity is the "stands" evidence the papers cite, but no measured input exists for any specific historic event.

### Gaps
- The friction angle used by Psycharis et al. (their Table I) did not survive text extraction; the EESD full text of Konstantinidis & Makris 2005 (column geometry, μ, record list) was not accessed; Drosos & Anastasopoulos model scale, drum count and the overturning amplitude are in the paywalled paper.
- No documented *collapse* of a real multidrum colonnade with recorded ground motion was found.

## KQ6. Historic towers and walls: Pavia 1989, St Mark's 1902, Noto 1996, Pisa (as negative controls and one "stands" case)

### Takeaway
All three collapses the question names are, per their investigators, long-term compression/creep failures of multi-leaf masonry — Pavia (2.8 m walls, 120,000 kN tower with a 30,000 kN granite belfry, 1.7–2.0 N/mm² computed stress against 1.5–3.5 N/mm² prism strength), Noto (rubble-cored piers cracked since the 1950s, "last blow" from the 1990 earthquake) and St Mark's (a 40 × 30 cm cut at the base, a crack up the north face, then a vertical collapse "on itself"). None toppled; a rigid-block engine without crushing should predict all three *stand*, and that is the right assertion. Pisa is the converse: a tower at 5.5° whose rigid-body overturning margin is enormous, and whose real hazard was soil "leaning instability".

### Cited Findings

#### Pavia Civic Tower, 17 March 1989
- "suddenly collapsed on 17 March 1989"; 4 killed; 7,000 m³ of ruins; hypotheses from settlement to bombs to sonic booms were tested — [Binda, Anzani & Saisi, WIT Trans. Built Env. 66, 2003 (PDF)](https://www.witpress.com/Secure/elibrary/papers/STR03/STR03010FU.pdf)
- Geometry/mass: built in phases from 1060; granite-block belfry added 1583–98; "the 2.8 m thick load-bearing walls were made of two thin external faces in regular brickwork and an internal sort of concrete made with layers of broken bricks and stone pebbles alternated with thick mortar joints"; a staircase inside the wall thickness; "total estimated weight of the Tower was 120,000 kN, while the weight of the belfry alone was 30,000 kN" — [Binda et al. 2003](https://www.witpress.com/Secure/elibrary/papers/STR03/STR03010FU.pdf)
- Stress vs strength: FE elastic model gave maximum compression 1.7–2.0 N/mm² in the lower part "against experimental strength values, obtained on 400 × 600 × 700 mm prisms, ranging between 1.5 and 3.5 N/mm²"; settlement and chemical damage ruled out; "The only clear situation was the high value of compressive stress due to the dead load"; creep tests on recovered prisms led to the creep-damage explanation — [Binda et al. 2003](https://www.witpress.com/Secure/elibrary/papers/STR03/STR03010FU.pdf); "progressive damage dating back many years and due mainly to the heavy dead load put on top of the existing medieval tower" — [Stavbaweb summary of Binda's 'Learning from Failure'](https://www.stavbaweb.cz/clanek/learning-from-failure-long-term-behaviour-of-heavy-masonry-structures-2456)

#### Noto Cathedral, 13 March 1996
- Partial collapse after damage in the 1990 earthquake; piers built with "external leaves in limestone and filled internally with rubble"; removal of 1960s plaster revealed "a diffused distribution of vertical cracks and spalling of the corners"; "the pillars were already damaged in the fifties by large vertical cracks due to high compression stresses"; the earthquake "was only worsening the situation"; collapse began at one pillar "and then propagated to... the other pillars"; remaining piers were demolished and rebuilt — [Binda et al. 2003](https://www.witpress.com/Secure/elibrary/papers/STR03/STR03010FU.pdf); [WIT record](https://www.witpress.com/elibrary/wit-transactions-on-the-built-environment/42/5622)

#### St Mark's Campanile, Venice, 14 July 1902
- Dimensions: total 98.6 m; brick shaft 12 m square and 50 m tall — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Mark%27s_Campanile)
- Trigger and progression: in July 1902 workers "cut a fissure about 40 cm high and 30 cm deep at the base of the tower to remove a girder supporting the loggetta roof"; on 7 July the shaft "trembled" as the new girder was hammered in; by 12 July "a large crack ran almost the full height of the northern side"; on 14 July the tell-tales were broken, the crack had widened up to 0.75 cm overnight; square evacuated 09:30; stones falling 09:47; total collapse 09:53 — [Wikipedia, citing Distefano and Zanetto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Mark%27s_Campanile)
- Mode: "The tower collapsed vertically rather than toppling. Investigators found that the access ramps between the inner and outer shafts fell one by one, starting from the upper levels. The outer shaft then caved in against the inner shaft" — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Mark%27s_Campanile); contemporaries: "collapsed on itself", debris cone >10 m high, basilica unharmed — [Marquis-Kyle, citing Pietragnoli 1992](https://www.marquis-kyle.com.au/mt/002109.php); the 1912 *American Architect* account rejected settlement because a sinking tower "would have tilted and fallen over as a whole rather than collapsing onto its own site" and blamed mortar "turned to dry dust" — search summary of [AMAR 22 May 1912](https://www.usmodernist.org/AMAR/AMAR-1912-05-22.pdf)
- Priors: 1511 earthquake opened "a long fissure on the northern side"; repeatedly struck by lightning (1388, 1489, 1745 etc.) — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Mark%27s_Campanile)

#### Leaning Tower of Pisa (stands)
- Foundations 19.6 m in diameter, maximum 5.5 m below ground; the foundation "sloped towards the south at 5.5 degrees to the horizontal, and by 1990 the seventh floor overhung the ground by about 4.5 m" — search summaries of [Burland et al., Int. J. Geoengineering Case Histories 1(3)](https://www.geocasehistoriesjournal.org/pub/article/download/IJGCH_1_3_2/86) and [Ingenia](https://ingenia.org.uk/articles/the-stabilisation-of-the-leaning-tower-of-pisa/) (both pages refused direct fetch)
- "Leaning instability": once the overturning moment from a small increase in inclination equals or exceeds the resisting moment from the foundations the structure becomes unstable; past a critical height "the smallest perturbation will induce leaning instability"; underexcavation reduced the inclination by about 10%; later rotation fell below 0.2 arcsec/yr (2003–08) — same sources ([Ingenia](https://ingenia.org.uk/articles/the-stabilisation-of-the-leaning-tower-of-pisa/); [ICE](https://www.ice.org.uk/what-is-civil-engineering/what-do-civil-engineers-do/stabilising-the-leaning-tower-of-pisa/))

### Inferences
- **Negative-control assertions.** For Pavia, model the tower as a rigid prism of 2.8 m walls with the 30,000 kN belfry on top on a rigid base: a rigid-block engine must report *stands* (no overturning or sliding demand exists); the real failure needed a compression-crushing/creep law at ~2 N/mm². For St Mark's, cut a 0.3 m deep × 0.4 m high notch into a 12 m square, 50 m shaft: a rigid-block engine must again report *stands* (the notch is 2.5% of the wall thickness of one face) — the real failure was internal-leaf disintegration. Noto likewise. Including these guards against a tempting but wrong reading of the catalogue: these towers did not fall by rigid-body statics.
- **Pisa "stands" assertion.** A ~58 m tower (height from general sources; not re-verified here) whose centroid is ~25 m up, tilted 5.5°, has its weight line offset ≈ 25 × sin 5.5° ≈ 2.4 m from the foundation centre against a 9.8 m half-width — far inside the base. A rigid-block engine must report stands with a large margin; the only path to failure is a yielding foundation, which is outside rigid-block scope. (My arithmetic.)

### Gaps
- Pavia tower height and plan were not in the extracted text (the paper's figures were images); the 1.7–2.0 N/mm² figure is from an elastic FE model, i.e. reconstructed.
- Pisa numbers come from search snippets of the Burland/Ingenia/ICE pages; the pages themselves returned 403.
- No "leaning-wall collapse attributed to statics" with published geometry was found other than Liberton (KQ1).

## KQ7. Retaining-wall and dry-stone collapses beyond Burgoyne 1834

### Takeaway
No documented *field* failure of a dry-stone retaining wall with published geometry and fill height (other than Burgoyne) was found; what exists are full-scale tests to failure — Colas, Morel & Garnier 2010 (two 2.5 m walls backfilled with gravel to failure) and the Bath EPSRC programme (Mundell, McCombie, Heath, Walker 2009; walls built and tested to destruction, bulging reproduced) — plus Southampton DEM work predicting that toppling of unweathered dry-stone walls is brittle, with crest deflection under 1% of backfill height before the factor of safety reaches ~1.05.

### Cited Findings
- Colas, Morel & Garnier, "Full-scale field trials to assess dry-stone retaining wall stability", *Engineering Structures* 32(5) 1215–1222, 2010, DOI 10.1016/j.engstruct.2009.12.047: two walls, each 2.5 m high, "loaded with gravel backfill until failure", to supply calibration data for yield-design models — [Coventry record](https://pureportal.coventry.ac.uk/en/publications/full-scale-field-trials-to-assess-dry-stone-retaining-wall-stabil/)
- Mundell, McCombie, Heath & Walker, "Drystone retaining walls: from full-scale testing to construction requirements", NOCMAT 2009, Bath: full-scale walls "constructed and tested to destruction as part of an EPSRC funded project"; "successfully recreated bulging"; analysis of "the critical aspects within a wall which instigate phenomena such as bursting or toppling" — [Bath record](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/drystone-retaining-walls-from-full-scale-testing-to-construction-)
- Southampton DEM (Harkness/Powrie et al., Géotechnique 2002): "toppling failure of unweathered drystone retaining walls is likely to occur in a brittle manner, with wall crest deflections not exceeding 1% of the backfill height before the factor of safety drops to about 1.05"; weathering and a compressible sub-base reduce the failure backfill height below limit-equilibrium; bulging associated with joint-stiffness deterioration — search summary of [Southampton eprint 39421](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/39421)
- Walker et al. 2005 rigid-block DEM parametric study of joint stiffness, tensile strength, block geometry and backfill; Oetomo et al. 2013 planar failure under excess backfill pressure compared with limestone and schist full-scale tests — [Coventry record](https://pureportal.coventry.ac.uk/en/publications/modeling-the-two-dimensional-failure-of-dry-stone-retaining-wall/); [HAL record](https://hal-genes.archives-ouvertes.fr/ENTPE/hal-02387647v1)
- Field collapses found are news items without geometry (Croughton allotment wall after prolonged rain; Wetley Rocks road retaining wall 2026) — [Croughton PC](https://croughtonparishcouncil.gov.uk/news/collapse-of-the-allotment-wall); [Staffordshire CC](https://www.staffordshire.gov.uk/newsroom/2026/repair-work-starts-collapsed-retaining-wall)
- BRE retaining-wall rules of thumb extend only to 1.725 m retained height — [LABC](https://labc.co.uk/news/how-building-regulations-may-affect-garden-walls)

### Inferences
- The Colas 2010 walls are the right validation target once the full paper is obtained: 2.5 m walls, known stone and gravel densities, backfill raised until failure; the assertion would be the failure fill height and whether the wall toppled about its toe or bulged/burst. Until then, only the mechanism statement (brittle toppling at <1% crest deflection) is assertable.

### Gaps
- Colas 2010 thicknesses, batter, stone/backfill densities, failure heights and mechanisms are in the paywalled paper; HAL returned a bot-challenge page.
- Bath test count, geometry and failure loads are in the Paper 181 PDF, not accessed.
- No real-world dry-stone *failure* with geometry was found.

## KQ8. Timber-on-masonry roof collapses after a wall plate or post was removed

### Takeaway
No investigation report of a roof collapse caused specifically by removal of a wall plate or post was found. The nearest documented events are the Windsor 2020 and Bushey 2021 HSE cases (roof and gable supports removed → gable overturned) and a UK structural survey attributing progressive gable distortion to a roof alteration that removed purlin dead load and created collar-tie thrust at the wall head; the mechanics literature confirms roof thrust destabilises a rocking wall while roof weight stabilises it at small rotations.

### Cited Findings
- "masonry portions supporting roofs may occur due to the roof thrust, which generates a destabilizing effect over motion. Nevertheless, the roof weight can produce a positive stabilizing effect for rotation amplitudes smaller than the critical value" — search summary of [Univ. Cagliari paper](https://iris.unica.it/handle/11584/189761)
- A roof alteration "remove[d] necessary dead load from gable walls that was in place via historic purlins and set up a loose (collar tie) rafter arrangement due to under specifying and poorly installing a timber ridge beam support, this has allowed the generation of thrust at the head of the walls. The wall is now severely distorted, and the movement is progressive" — [Ribble Valley structural survey 22/0317](https://webportal.ribblevalley.gov.uk/planx_downloads/22_0317_Structural_Survey_Report2.pdf)
- Windsor 2020 and Bushey 2021: gables fell after roofs and their supporting timbers/steels were removed (details under KQ2, Cases 2.6–2.7) — [Construction Index](https://www.theconstructionindex.co.uk/news/view/suspended-prison-sentence-for-builder-who-brought-the-house-down); [HSM](https://www.hsmsearch.com/?p=17479)
- Timber-truss guidance case: an internal wall removed and a hanging beam hung from the rafters left a girder truss "supporting approximately 10m of roof applied as two large point" loads — [MiTek GN50](https://www.mitek.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/GN-Guideline-50.pdf)
- L'Aquila: "collapse of the central zone of a wall determined by the horizontal action of the wooden beam" (roof beam pushing the wall out) — [ReLUIS report](https://www.reluis.it/doc/pdf/Aquila/Report_masonry_building_1.pdf)

### Inferences
- The assertable physics is: (a) a gable wall restrained at its head by purlins/roof stands under wind; remove the roof and the same wall is a free-standing cantilever whose overturning threshold is t/h of the full gable (Windsor/Bushey outcome: falls); (b) a wall carrying roof weight through a wall plate has a larger overturning margin than the bare wall (weight adds to W in F = W·t/2h) until rafter thrust is added, which subtracts; a test can take a generic 215 mm, 5 m gable, add 10 kN/m of roof load with and without a horizontal thrust component, and assert the margin moves in the documented directions. No case supplies the numbers.

### Gaps
- No HSE/NIOSH/FAI report on a roof collapse caused by wall-plate or post removal was found in this search; this question is the least served by published investigations.
