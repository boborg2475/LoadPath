Lintel wall, joint stress

Test 3.13: a dry 10-course running-bond wall (μ 0.7) over a 1.075 m opening, with its timber lintel removed. The wall is always drawn standing; bricks that are gone in a state are drawn as outlines. Joint readings are the elastic solver's (RBSM). Motion arrows show either the certificate's chosen mechanism or the elastic solver's own free motion.

Why the engine releases 34 bricks instead of 10
  1. The certificate decides which bricks drop, not the elastic solver. The combined authority copies the certificate's moving bodies into Release (CombinedAuthority.cpp:396). The cascade then drops that whole list in one pass.
  2. λ ≈ 0, so every falling motion is equally "right". Dry joints absorb no work, so any admissible motion in which gravity does work proves collapse. The certificate breaks the tie by taking the motion with the most gravity work per unit of body motion. It lists every body that moves more than 1/1000 of the largest.
  3. The certificate's friction is associative: a sliding joint must open by μ × slip. The triangle dropping straight down slides 1 along each of its 8 side joints while opening 0, and the rule needs 0.7. So its neighbours have to spread apart and ride up their beds (c6x0.50 moves (−0.122, +0.086), a ratio of 0.705 ≈ μ). All 58 sliding joints in the chosen mechanism open by at least μ × slip.
  4. The same rule gives a false "stands" at higher friction. At μ ≥ 1.0 the certificate reads Stands at λ = 160 and the cascade returns Undecided. At μ 0.05–0.7 it releases 31–34 bricks.

Why the elastic solver alone releases only 2
  1. It finds one set of loose bricks at a time. With the lintel gone, its active set frees only c6x3.50 and c6x4.50, the two middle bricks of course 6. Every joint around them is open, so they drop straight down (Mechanism).
  2. On its next pass it stops. The other 8 triangle bricks are still held in its last solved field, for two reasons. Part of it is friction from an arch squeeze: about 165 N pushes across the middle of courses 6–9, and the apex joint in the top course carries 282 N. The rest is tension in dry bed joints, which is impossible: c7x4.00 hangs 24.6 N of its 28 N weight from the bricks above it. When the solver opens those corners, the next loose motion would push open corners shut. It cannot restore them, so it reads Undecided and releases nothing more.
  3. It never said the triangle stands. It reads Undecided even on the intact wall, where the two top-corner half bricks come loose the same way. The intact verdict is Stands only because the certificate proves it (λ 413).

Should the triangle fall?
  1. Nothing holds it from below. The course-6 triangle bricks sit over the void, and dry joints carry no tension. Only friction on their side joints can hold them, and that needs a sideways squeeze, which only an arch across the opening can provide.
  2. The arch needs abutments that resist its thrust. Here the abutments are the stepped shoulders above the piers. Each of their bed joints can resist at most μ × the weight above it, and the upper shoulder courses weigh little. The top course of one shoulder weighs about 105 N, so it resists at most 74 N, against the 282 N apex squeeze in the solver's own field.
  3. The certificate settles it. With the piers fixed and the shoulders free, it still reads Collapses at μ 0.7. With the shoulders fixed too, the triangle stands (λ 160). So the shoulders are the weak link. Associative friction is the generous rule: the real, non-associative collapse load can be no higher (Radenkovic's theorem). So if even the generous rule collapses, the real wall does too. The answer depends on μ: at μ 1.0 the same check stands.
  4. "Exactly 10" is too strong. With the triangle removed beforehand, the certificate still collapses the shoulders (λ ≈ 0), so some shoulder corbels must fall too. The test rightly leaves them unasserted. It asserts only that the 10 fall and the 4 bricks wholly on the piers stay. The basis text says "exactly", and that needs a ruling.
