# Numerical benchmark suites for rigid-block statics and limit analysis

Scope: published verification problems, with reference answers, used to validate rigid-block limit-analysis codes (LP/SOCP/MPEC), discrete-element codes (3DEC/UDEC, LMGC90, Chrono) and open-source block-equilibrium tools. All numbers are quoted verbatim from the cited source; units are stated per problem (several canonical sets are deliberately dimensionless). "Flow rule" flags mark problems whose answer depends on associative (dilatant, φ₀ = φ) versus non-associative (zero-dilatancy, φ₀ = 0) Coulomb friction.

Sources were obtained as follows: the Ferris & Tin-Loi report and the two Orduña & Lourenço papers were downloaded and read in full (the Ferris report had to be rasterised page-by-page because its Type-3 fonts defeat text extraction); the Iannuzzo thesis, Forgács et al., Sarhosis et al. and Cocchetti et al. were read from full text; Itasca 3DEC verification pages were read directly; Gilbert et al. 2006, Livesley 1978, Baggio & Trovalusci 1998, Portioli et al. 2014 and Kao et al. 2022 were paywalled or blocked and are reported from abstracts, Crossref metadata and secondary reproductions only.

## KQ1. Livesley (1978) and Livesley (1992): the founding LP formulation and its examples

### Takeaway
Livesley 1978 is the origin of the "maximise the load factor subject to equilibrium plus linear interface failure constraints" formulation; Part I assumes shear capacity independent of normal force (so normality holds and the bound theorems apply) and describes an arch-collapse program, Part II switches to Coulomb friction and warns that the computed load may then be an over-estimate. The actual example geometries and load values are behind the Wiley paywall and were not recoverable from any open mirror.

### Cited Findings
- Full citation and abstract (Crossref): R.K. Livesley, "Limit analysis of structures formed from rigid blocks", International Journal for Numerical Methods in Engineering 12 (1978) 1853–1871, DOI 10.1002/nme.1620121207. Abstract: "In this procedure the load factor is maximized subject to the equilibrium equations of the structure and linear constraints imposed by criteria of failure at the block interfaces. In part I of the paper it is assumed that the limiting shear force associated with sliding at a block interface is independent of the normal component of force across the interface. This assumption means that the normality rule is satisfied, so that the upper- and lower-bound theorems of classical limit analysis apply. This part includes a description of computer program for the collapse analysis of masonry arches with joints incapable of carrying tension. In part II the limit on the shear force at a block interface is assumed to be that associated with Coulomb friction ... the failure mechanism computed by the algorithm will not necessarily satisfy the normality rule. The corresponding limit load may therefore be an over-estimate of the true failure load, even though it is computed by a lower-bound (equilibrium) approach. A criterion is established for testing the validity of a failure load computed in these circumstances." — [Crossref record for DOI 10.1002/nme.1620121207](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.1620121207)
- Livesley's 3-D extension: R.K. Livesley, "A computational model for the limit analysis of three-dimensional masonry structures", Meccanica 27(3) (1992) 161–172, DOI 10.1007/BF00430042 — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00430042); [Springer volume listing](https://www.springer.com/journal/11012/volumes-and-issues/27-3)
- Orduña & Lourenço (Part II, Discussion) note that the piecewise-linear interface model "overestimates the strengths for a number of stress states (Orduña and Lourenço, 2005). Livesley (1992)" is cited in the same breath as the precedent for 3-D torsion/shear interaction — [Orduña & Lourenço 2005 Part II, p. 5178](https://hdl.handle.net/1822/4600)
- A MATLAB program "Arch Stability" implementing Livesley's 1978 methodology for arch rigid-body assemblies exists in later literature (secondary mention only) — [search summary citing arch-bridges.fzu.edu.cn PDF](https://arch-bridges.fzu.edu.cn/__local/B/A9/86/18C4CC04C8DE04A02E922E606ED_D4814A94_5A92F.pdf?e=.pdf)
- Ferris & Tin-Loi list Livesley 1978 and 1992 as references [5] and [6] and cite the Coulomb-friction/normality problem exactly as Livesley framed it — [Ferris & Tin-Loi TR 99-01, p. 14](https://minds.wisconsin.edu/bitstream/handle/1793/64402/99-01.pdf)

### Inferences
- Any new engine that solves the associative LP should reproduce Livesley Part I by construction; the useful test is Livesley's Part II criterion (does the mechanism's sliding direction violate normality?), because that is the diagnostic every later non-associative method is built around.
- Because the 1978 example numbers are unavailable, the Ferris & Tin-Loi wall set (KQ2) is the practical substitute: it is the oldest openly published table of 2-D rigid-block load factors.

### Gaps
- The specific geometry, loads and collapse loads of Livesley's 1978 arch and wall examples were not found in any open source; the Wiley full text is paywalled and no secondary paper quotes the numbers.
- Livesley 1992's 3-D examples were likewise not retrievable.

## KQ2. Baggio & Trovalusci (1998/2000) and Ferris & Tin-Loi (2001): the classic 2-D wall benchmark set with associative vs non-associative load factors

### Takeaway
The Ferris & Tin-Loi set is the single best-documented known-answer suite: six 2-D running-bond walls (33 to 146 blocks) in dimensionless units, each solved three ways (associative LP, non-associative MCP, non-associative MPEC), with load factors quoted to five significant figures. The non-associative answer is 0.6 % to 25.9 % lower than the associative one, so every problem in the set is flow-rule sensitive.

### Cited Findings
- Bibliographic: F. Tin-Loi & M.C. Ferris, "Limit analysis of frictional block assemblies as a mathematical program with complementarity constraints", Technical Report 99-01, 15 Feb 1999 (open), later International Journal of Mechanical Sciences 43(1) (2001) 209–224, DOI 10.1016/S0020-7403(99)00111-3 — [MINDS@UW record](https://minds.wisc.edu/items/fbb167fd-be6a-4521-bfef-dbf4961b8c8e/full); [PDF](https://minds.wisconsin.edu/bitstream/handle/1793/64402/99-01.pdf); [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0020-7403(99)00111-3)
- Problem definition, quoted: "Basic details of all models are: blocks of (full) size 4 × 1.75 and (half) size 2 × 1.75; φ = tan⁻¹ 0.65; ψ = 45°; φ₀ = φ (associated, for LP problems) or φ₀ = 0° (nonassociated, for MCP and MPEC problems). All blocks are subjected to vertical (downward) self weight and horizontal (left to right) live loads, simulating an earthquake-type loading. In particular, for each j-th full block f_D^jT = (0, −1, 0) and f_L^jT = (α, 0, 0); and hence for each j-th half block f_D^jT = (0, −1/2, 0) and f_L^jT = (α/2, 0, 0)." The models are "two-dimensional block assemblages such as those considered by Baggio and Trovalusci [13]" and the six configurations are "very similar to those in [13]". — [TR 99-01, p. 11](https://minds.wisconsin.edu/bitstream/handle/1793/64402/99-01.pdf)
- Table 1 "Computational results" (b = blocks, c = contacts; α = limit load factor; secs = CPU on a 333 MHz Pentium-II):

- Interpretation quoted: "The limit loads obtained by solving an MPEC are generally smaller than the corresponding MCP formulation. In turn, the MCP results are smaller than those given by the LP formulation which assumes associativity." "The assumption of associativity (in the LP problems) produced higher collapse loads (up to about 25.9% higher for Example 4)". - Geometry of the configurations, from Figs 4–8 (collapse mechanisms, nonassociated): Example 2 is a solid running-bond wall 5 full blocks wide by 11 courses (55 blocks) failing by a stepped diagonal crack with the right-hand part overturning; Example 3 is a wall with a single central opening (two piers joined by lintel and sill courses, 46 blocks); Example 4 is a wall with two openings side by side (55 blocks); Example 5 is a slender pier with two openings stacked vertically (61 blocks); Example 6 is a larger wall with four openings in a 2 × 2 pattern (146 blocks). Fig. 3 (Example 1) did not render in the PDF; only its caption value α = 0.63898 is visible. — [TR 99-01, pp. 19–23](https://minds.wisconsin.edu/bitstream/handle/1793/64402/99-01.pdf)
- Solvers used: "GAMS/CPLEX (version 6.0) to solve the LP problem (8), GAMS/PATH (version 4.0) to solve the MCP (7) and GAMS/CONOPT2 (version 2.070F) to solve the NLP problem (11)". The MPEC relaxation sets "initial μ (e.g. 10⁻³)", exits when "yᵀz ≤ 10⁻¹⁰", and halves μ each iteration. — [TR 99-01, pp. 9, 11](https://minds.wisconsin.edu/bitstream/handle/1793/64402/99-01.pdf)
- Baggio & Trovalusci, "Limit analysis for no-tension and frictional three-dimensional discrete systems", Mechanics of Structures and Machines 26(3) (1998) 287–304, DOI 10.1080/08905459708945496 — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1080/08905459708945496); Orduña & Lourenço describe it: "Baggio and Trovalusci (1998) proposed to minimise the load factor in a rigid blocks limit analysis formulation ... Nevertheless, those authors faced serious difficulties in the solution phase." — [Orduña & Lourenço 2005 Part II, p. 5162](https://hdl.handle.net/1822/4600)
- Baggio & Trovalusci, "Collapse behaviour of three-dimensional brick-block systems using non-linear programming", Structural Engineering and Mechanics 10(2) (2000) 181–195, DOI 10.12989/sem.2000.10.2.181. Method: an LP "treating interfaces as no-tension and dilatant" seeds "a subsequent non-linear, non-convex problem incorporating friction", solved by SQP. — [KoreaScience record](http://koreascience.or.kr/journal/view.jsp?kj=KJKHB9&py=2000&vnc=v10n2&sp=181)
- Ferris & Tin-Loi describe the computational results as "particularly encouraging especially in view of the difficulties encountered for similar problems by Baggio and Trovalusci [13]" — [TR 99-01, p. 12](https://minds.wisconsin.edu/bitstream/handle/1793/64402/99-01.pdf)
- Trentadue & Quaranta re-solve frictional block assemblies by a fictitious associative-type contact law and report "numerical results concerning masonry arches, portals and panels" — F. Trentadue, G. Quaranta, "Limit analysis of frictional block assemblies by means of fictitious associative-type contact interface laws", Int. J. Mech. Sci. 70 (2013) 140–145, DOI 10.1016/j.ijmecsci.2013.02.012 — [IRIS Sapienza record](https://iris.uniroma1.it/handle/11573/514707)

### Inferences
- The Ferris & Tin-Loi set is dimensionless: block width 4 and height 1.75 with unit weight 1 per full block. A reproduction in SI only needs consistent scaling (e.g. 0.40 m × 0.175 m blocks of any density) because α is a ratio of horizontal to vertical mass-proportional loads.
- The "ψ = 45°" parameter is the slope of the rocking limit surface in the (n, m̄) plane shown in Fig. 2, i.e. the moment capacity is tied to the normal force by the block half-width; a reproducing engine must use the same contact-point/moment convention or the associative LP values will drift.
- Example 1 (0.6 % difference) is the natural "flow rule barely matters" control case; Example 4 (25.9 %) is the natural "flow rule matters a lot" case. A referee that solves the associative LP correctly must hit the LP column; a non-associative engine should land between the MPEC and LP columns (the MPEC is a local minimum heuristic, so lower values are theoretically possible).
- Several independent codes have revisited this set (Gilbert et al. 2006 "problems from the literature", Orduña & Lourenço's comparison against "the procedure proposed by Ferris and Tin-Loi (2001)", Trentadue & Quaranta 2013), which makes it the most-trusted 2-D suite, but only the Ferris & Tin-Loi numbers themselves were verifiable here.

### Gaps
- Baggio & Trovalusci's own 1998/2000 load multipliers and exact wall drawings were not accessible (paywalled journals); Ferris & Tin-Loi say their geometries are only "very similar", so they are not a strict cross-check.
- The exact layout of Example 1 (33 blocks) is missing because Fig. 3 is blank in the PDF rendering.

## KQ3. Gilbert, Casapulla & Ahmed (2006) and Gilbert & Melbourne (1994) / RING-type arch problems

### Takeaway
Gilbert et al. 2006 is the standard iterative-LP (modified Mohr–Coulomb cone, updated each iteration) treatment of non-associative friction, validated on "problems from the literature" and "new, considerably larger, benchmark problems"; its numeric tables are paywalled. The RING lineage rests on full-scale arch tests (Bolton, Salford, Bargower) whose collapse loads are published in reviews.

### Cited Findings
- M. Gilbert, C. Casapulla, H.M. Ahmed, "Limit analysis of masonry block structures with non-associative frictional joints using linear programming", Computers & Structures 84(13–14) (2006) 873–887, DOI 10.1016/j.compstruc.2006.02.005. Method: "a simple iterative procedure ... which involves the successive solution of simple linear programming sub-problems, using a specially modified Mohr-Coulomb failure surface at each contact interface, with all failure surfaces updated at each iteration until a converged solution is obtained. The procedure is applied to problems from the literature and also to new, considerably larger, benchmark problems." — [IRIS UNINA record](https://iris.unina.it/handle/11588/300870); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compstruc.2006.02.005)
- Semantic Scholar confirms the paper is "CLOSED" access with abstract elided by the publisher — [Semantic Scholar API record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1016/j.compstruc.2006.02.005?fields=title,year,openAccessPdf)
- Portioli, Cascini, D'Aniello & Landolfo (Civil-Comp 2012) state "To take into account the nonassociative behaviour in sliding, the iterative solution procedure proposed by Gilbert et al. was used to find the minimum collapse load" and validate on "a literature case study of unconfined masonry panel, where the collapse mechanism involves cracking of units" — [Civil-Comp CST2012 summary](https://webapp.tudelft.nl/proceedings/cst2012/html/summary/portioli.htm)
- Portioli, Casapulla, Gilbert & Cascini, "Limit analysis of 3D masonry block structures with non-associative frictional joints using cone programming", Computers & Structures 143 (2014) 108–121, DOI 10.1016/j.compstruc.2014.07.010: SOCP with a concave contact model, "validation against numerical benchmark problems and experimental tests on masonry walls and buildings"; no open PDF. — [IRIS UNINA record](https://www.iris.unina.it/handle/11588/584309)
- M. Gilbert & C. Melbourne, "Rigid-block analysis of masonry structures", The Structural Engineer 72(21) (1994) 356–360: "uses the upper-bound theory of plasticity in conjunction with geometrical compatibility criteria to obtain solutions to problems involving single- and multi-span arches; well established rigorous linear programming methods are used ... ring separation and attached spandrel walls can be modelled" — [IStructE journal page](https://www.istructe.org/journal/volumes/volume-72-(published-in-1994)/issue-21/rigid-block-analysis-of-masonry-structures)
- Full-scale arch test data used by that lineage (from the Sarhosis, De Santis & de Felice review): "Melbourne and Walker (1990) carried out a full scale model test on 6m span brickwork arch ... The maximum load was 100 kN on the arch with fill and no spandrel walls, 150 kN with unrestrained spandrel walls, and, finally, 320 kN with restrained spandrel walls." Bolton small-scale arches "had 3 m span, 0.75 m rise and 21.5 cm thickness (one brick)"; "The Bargower masonry arch had a span of 10m, rise at mid span equal to 5.18m"; "Melbourne and Gilbert (1995) carried out six large scale model tests on brickwork masonry arch" bridges (ring separation). — [Sarhosis et al., White Rose eprint 146294](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/146294/)
- Fishwick & Begg validated an automatic LP rigid-block method against a segmental concrete block arch tested to destruction: "the model significantly overestimated the test structure's strength" until "minor imperfections in the concrete blocks" were measured and included. R.J. Fishwick & D.W. Begg, "Validation of an automatic limit analysis technique for rigid block systems", WIT Trans. Modelling and Simulation 17 (1997), DOI 10.2495/CMEM970121, open access. — [WIT Press](https://www.witpress.com/elibrary/wit-transactions-on-modelling-and-simulation/17/7680)
- Ferris & Tin-Loi thank "Dr. R.J. Fishwick (University of Portsmouth) for kindly providing us with a copy of his PhD thesis" (R.J. Fishwick, Limit analysis of rigid block structures, PhD, Portsmouth, 1996) — [TR 99-01, pp. 13–14](https://minds.wisconsin.edu/bitstream/handle/1793/64402/99-01.pdf)

### Inferences
- Gilbert et al. 2006 almost certainly re-solved the Ferris & Tin-Loi walls (they are "the problems from the literature" for 2-D non-associative friction in 2006), so an engine matching the Ferris & Tin-Loi MPEC column is implicitly matching Gilbert's iterative LP; but this could not be confirmed numerically.
- The arch-bridge experiments are integration tests (fill, spandrels, multi-ring), not clean rigid-block unit tests; the Fishwick & Begg result is a warning that geometric imperfection, not the solver, dominates experimental mismatch for dry block arches.

### Gaps
- No open copy of Gilbert et al. 2006's tables (wall sizes, μ, load factors per iteration) was found; Elsevier linkinghub and the DOI both redirect to a paywall.
- Portioli et al. 2014's benchmark table (which should list 3-D cases against Orduña & Lourenço and Ferris & Tin-Loi) was not accessible.

## KQ4. Orduña & Lourenço (2005) Parts I and II: 3-D rigid-block limit analysis, torsion, and the load-path-following validation set

### Takeaway
Part I supplies the torsion/shear/bending interface yield model; Part II supplies three open-access 3-D validation cases (hollow masonry pile, two out-of-plane walls) with SI inputs, FEM comparators, and a direct comparison of "minimise the load factor" (Ferris & Tin-Loi style) versus load-path following. The pile case has a closed theoretical range for the non-associative load factor (0.427 to 0.553), which makes it an unusually good referee problem.

### Cited Findings
- A. Orduña & P.B. Lourenço, "Three-dimensional limit analysis of rigid blocks assemblages. Part I: Torsion failure on frictional interfaces and limit analysis formulation", Int. J. Solids Struct. 42(18) (2005) 5140–5160, DOI 10.1016/j.ijsolstr.2005.02.010; Part II: "load-path following solution procedure and validation", 42 (2005) 5161–5180, DOI 10.1016/j.ijsolstr.2005.02.011; both green OA at Minho — [Part I handle](https://hdl.handle.net/1822/4601); [Part II handle](https://hdl.handle.net/1822/4600)
- Part I scope, quoted: "the governing equations of plastic torsion of arbitrarily shaped frictional interfaces" with "the particular case of rectangular interfaces"; the interaction curves are plotted for "l1 = 0.15 m, l2 = 0.10 m, μ = 0.7" (Fig. 8, Fig. 12). "In the particular case of friction with zero dilatancy, as is the case of concern of this paper" — [Part I](https://hdl.handle.net/1822/4601)
- Non-uniqueness statement: "In general, there is no unique solution for this problem in the presence of non-associated flow rules. If the load factor is minimised, as proposed by Baggio and Trovalusci (1998), the solution can severely underestimate the ultimate load factor". Load-path following parameter: "recommended values, according to the authors experience, are 1.1 ≤ f ≤ 1.4." — [Part II, pp. 5165–5166](https://hdl.handle.net/1822/4600)
- **Case A, SW30 dry-stone shear wall (2-D, from Oliveira 2003 tests):** "The wall size was 1000 × 1000 mm, with a thickness of 200 mm. The mean compressive strength ... was 57.1 N/mm². The corresponding effective compressive stress ... is 23.7 N/mm²" with "f_cef = ν_e f_c, ν_e = 0.7 − f_c/200". "The measured friction coefficient value was 0.66". "The SW30 series consisted of two specimens with 30 kN of vertical load." FEM (DIANA) force–displacement points: δ (mm) → F (kN): 0.5 → 6.0; 1.0 → 10.1; 1.5 → 12.1; 2.0 → 13.3; 3.0 → 14.3; 15.0 → 16.3; "the FEM curve approaches asymptotically to the limit analysis load." — [Part II, pp. 5166–5169](https://hdl.handle.net/1822/4600)
- **Case B, Vasconcelos cyclic shear wall:** "These walls were 1.2 m high, 1.0 m wide and 0.2 m thick ... The block dimensions were 0.15 × 0.20 × 0.20 m. The measured stone volumetric weight is 25.7 kN/m³. For analysis purposes, a 0.7 friction coefficient is assumed ... infinite compressive strength ... A vertical constant load amounting to 250 kN is applied ... The distance between the specimen's top and the line of horizontal load action is 0.125 m." Experimental peaks: 81.6 kN (one direction, Fig. 10b), "the maximum experimental load for the opposite displacement direction was 92.8 kN and ... the mean value for the four specimens and two directions is 91.6 kN"; limit analysis F = 95.2 kN (Fig. 10e, infinite effective stress) and 76.1 kN at f_cef = 6.6 N/mm². — [Part II, pp. 5168–5170](https://hdl.handle.net/1822/4600)
- **Case C, hollow masonry pile (3-D, SI):** "The pile is built of dry masonry blocks with dimensions 0.2 × 0.2 × 0.4 m. The pile dimensions are 0.6 × 0.8 × 1.2 m. The material volumetric weight is 20 kN/m³ and the friction coefficient is 0.7. The permanent loads are the blocks self-weights. The variable loads are proportional to the blocks weight, but horizontally applied in the direction of the larger base side (X direction)." FEM interface data (Table 2): E = 1.0 kN/mm², ν = 0.2, k_n = 2.4 × 10³ N/mm³, k_s = 1.0 × 10³ N/mm³, μ = 0.7, ψ = 0. Results (Table 3 and text, infinite compressive strength): "If the reaction on the overturning blocks set is concentrated on the interface A only ... the ultimate load factor would be 0.427, the minimum possible for this mechanism ... the maximum possible ultimate load factor for the mechanism shown equals 0.553. The ultimate load factor calculated by minimising the load factor by the procedure proposed by Ferris and Tin-Loi (2001) is 0.427, equal to the minimum possible and 10.8% lower than the factor obtained by FEM. The ultimate load factor calculated with the load-path following procedure is 4.4% higher than the FEM value." Table 3 values: theoretical minimum 0.427, theoretical maximum 0.553, FEM 0.479, load-path following 0.500, minimising α 0.427. — [Part II, pp. 5172–5175](https://hdl.handle.net/1822/4600)
- **Case D, out-of-plane wall supported on one vertical edge:** "The structure is 1.053 m high, 0.630 m wide and 0.071 m thick, being the block dimensions equal to 0.081 × 0.210 × 0.07 m (height × length × thickness). The volumetric weight is 20 kN/m³, the friction coefficient is 0.7 and an infinite compressive strength is assumed." Load factors (Fig. 13): FEM α = 0.210; load-path limit analysis α = 0.216; minimising α (Ferris & Tin-Loi procedure) α = 0.127, which "differs 40% from the FEM value" and gives a "very different" mechanism. — [Part II, pp. 5176–5177](https://hdl.handle.net/1822/4600)
- **Case E, same wall "twice wide, 1.260 m", constrained at both vertical edges:** FEM α = 0.260; load-path α = 0.285 ("9.5% higher"); minimising α = 0.193 ("25.8% lower than the FEM one"). — [Part II, pp. 5177–5178](https://hdl.handle.net/1822/4600)
- General trend: "the load-path following procedure always gives slightly larger ultimate load factors than the FEM calculations ... attributed to the fact that the piecewise linear yield function used in the limit analysis interface model overestimates the strengths for a number of stress states" — [Part II, p. 5178](https://hdl.handle.net/1822/4600)

### Inferences
- The pile (Case C) is the best 3-D unit test found: 36 blocks at most (0.6 × 0.8 × 1.2 m of 0.2 × 0.2 × 0.4 m units), closed-form bracket [0.427, 0.553] for the non-associative answer, FEM at 0.479. A "minimum load factor" engine must return 0.427; an engine with any physically motivated selection rule should land inside the bracket.
- Cases D and E demonstrate the practical danger of the minimum-load-factor convention (40 % and 25.8 % below FEM); if the new engine adopts "minimise α", it should expect to under-predict these.
- Case B's caption value "f_cef = 1" is almost certainly a garbled "∞" (infinite compressive strength) in the text extraction; treat F = 95.2 kN as the infinite-strength limit load.

### Gaps
- The exact limit-analysis load for the SW30 wall (Case A) is only given graphically (Fig. 4b, 9); the text extraction did not recover the number.
- No independent code was found openly re-running Cases C–E with published numbers (Portioli et al. 2014 likely did, but is paywalled).

## KQ5. Verification sets of DEM codes (3DEC/UDEC, LMGC90, Chrono; Lemos reviews) and open-source rigid-block LP/equilibrium tools (LiABlock_3D, COMPAS)

### Takeaway
Itasca's 3DEC ships a documented verification suite with closed-form comparisons (sliding wedge, sliding/toppling block, falling wedge, etc.) plus masonry example applications (arch bridge, Flemish-bond wall, flying buttress) that are demonstrations rather than benchmarks. LMGC90 and Chrono publications validate qualitatively against arch shaking-table tests. COMPAS CRA/RBE ships example assemblies but no published reference answers in the repository; LiABlock_3D is validated against literature experiments.

### Cited Findings
- 3DEC verification problems (9.5 docs): "Sliding Wedge; Falling Wedge; Block with a Slipping Crack under Cyclic Loading; Cylindrical Hole in an Infinite Elastic Medium; Cylindrical Hole in an Infinite Mohr-Coulomb Medium; Rough Square Footing on a Cohesive Frictionless Material; Hydraulic Fracture; Sliding and Toppling Blocks; Pumping Test in a Single Horizontal Fracture; Uniaxial Compressive Strength of a Jointed Material Sample". These "are tests in which the 3DEC solution is compared directly to an analytical (i.e., closed-form) solution." — [3DEC Examples index](https://docs.itascacg.com/itasca950/3dec/docproject/source/examples/examples.html)
- **Sliding Wedge (closed form):** two planes "dip = 40° dip direction = 130°" and "dip = 60° dip direction = 220°"; "ρ = 2000 kg/m³"; cohesionless; "K_n = K_s = 10 MPa/m"; "g = 10 m/sec²". Safety factor F = ((R_A + R_B) tan φ)/(W sin ψ); analytical critical friction angle "φ = 33.36°", 3DEC "φ = 33.12°", error "0.7%". — [3DEC Sliding Wedge](https://docs.itascacg.com/itasca950/3dec/docproject/source/examples/SlidingWedge.html)

### Inferences
- Of the DEM sets, only the 3DEC Sliding Wedge and Sliding/Toppling Blocks have closed-form answers that a statics engine can reproduce exactly (friction angle at incipient sliding; regime classification by friction and aspect ratio). The arch bridge "6000 kN" is a code-specific dynamic result with joint stiffness, not a reference answer.
- The COMPAS example scripts fix geometry but the expected outputs live in the Kao et al. paper (403-blocked here); the repository therefore gives reproducible inputs, not reference answers.
- Chrono's masonry work uses associative friction by construction (DVI), so its collapse loads should match the associative LP column of an LP referee, not the non-associative one.

## KQ6. Published benchmark collections / round-robin exercises for masonry block analysis

### Takeaway
No formal rigid-block "benchmark collection" with a maintained reference table was found. What exists are (a) blind-prediction competitions on shaking-table specimens where rigid-block limit analysis was one of several methods, and (b) de-facto suites inherited from papers (Ferris & Tin-Loi walls, Orduña & Lourenço 3-D cases, Couplet–Heyman arches).

### Cited Findings
- SERA-AIMS project "Seismic Response of Masonry Cross Vaults: Shaking table tests and numerical validations" organised a blind prediction competition "comparing various numerical techniques, including 3D rigid block limit analysis" — [IRIS UNINA record (LiABlock_3D cross vault)](https://iris.unina.it/handle/11588/838331); [Structurae listing](https://structurae.de/de/fachliteratur/fachartikel/3d-rigid-block-limit-analysis-of-a-masonry-cross-vault-subjected-to-shaking-table-tests)
- A blind prediction of "a masonry building composed of two adjacent interacting structural units under earthquake excitation" was analysed "using a numerical model based on rigid block limit analysis and mathematical programming", and a follow-up URM benchmark exercise compared teams "in terms of capacity curves, predicted failure mechanisms, and related minimum values of peak ground acceleration (PGA)" — [IRIS UNINA record](https://iris.unina.it/handle/11588/985457); [EPFL Infoscience record](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/285869)
- A "benchmarking study of the analysis of non-reinforced structures applied to the structural behavior of domes" exists (Universidad de Navarra portal), comparing methods on masonry domes — [Navarra portal](https://portalcientifico.unav.edu/documentos/638be871840d3a6d9ac7da5f?lang=en)
- Searches for NIKER or PERPETUATE project benchmark sets for rigid-block analysis returned no hits: "the search results don't contain specific references to 'NIKER' or 'PERPETUATE'" — [search result summary](https://www.iitk.ac.in/nicee/wcee/article/WCEE2012_4233.pdf)

### Inferences
- The blind-prediction exercises report PGA and capacity curves across heterogeneous methods; they are not usable as sharp known-answer tests for a statics engine, but they are the closest thing to a round-robin that includes rigid-block LP.

### Gaps
- No NIKER/PERPETUATE rigid-block benchmark document was found; either it does not exist in that form or it is not indexed.
- The "Masonry structures: between mechanics and architecture" examples were not located.

## KQ7. The simplest canonical problems (block on incline, stacks, arches, portal) and closed-form arch benchmarks, with flow-rule sensitivity

### Takeaway
Closed-form single-block incline criteria (3DEC) and the Couplet–Heyman/Milankovitch minimum-thickness arch (t/R = 0.1075 with radial joints; 0.1135 when normalised by the intrados radius) are the canonical zero-ambiguity tests; Iannuzzo's thesis adds closed-form horizontal-load multipliers for circular arches (λ = 0.340, 0.392, 0.549). Published load factors for a two-block stack, three-block arch or four-block portal with both flow rules were not found; the smallest published flow-rule-sensitive cases are Orduña's pile and Ferris & Tin-Loi's 33-block wall.

### Cited Findings
- **Single block on a tilted base (closed form, 3DEC verification):** with slope 40°, width 10 m: stable for h = 10 m, φ = 40.1°; slides for h = 5 m, φ = 20°; topples for h = 25 m, φ = 40°; slides and topples for h = 20 m, φ = 30°. "Stability criteria derive from Wyllie and Mah (2004), comparing friction angle (φ) against slope angle (ψ_p)" and block aspect ratio. — [3DEC Sliding and Toppling Blocks](https://docs.itascacg.com/itasca950/3dec/docproject/source/examples/SlidingBlock.html)
- **Tetrahedral wedge on two planes (closed form):** critical φ = 33.36° analytical vs 33.12° numerical (details in KQ5) — [3DEC Sliding Wedge](https://docs.itascacg.com/itasca950/3dec/docproject/source/examples/SlidingWedge.html)
- **Couplet–Heyman minimum thickness of a semicircular arch under self-weight (closed form, no friction failure):** "Couplet ... minimum thickness tmin is equal to 0.101 R" with hinges at 45°; "Heyman (1977) ... found that for a semi-circular arch, θ_cr is equal to 58.82° and tmin is equal to 0.105965 · R"; "A more accurate approach derived from Milankovitch (1907), found that θ is equal to 54.48° and t_min is equal to 0.107478 · R (Alexakis and Makris 2013; Ochsendorf 2002)". — [Forgács, Sarhosis & Bagi 2017, White Rose eprint 144597](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/144597/)
- Stereotomy dependence: "Milankovitch's solution of t/R = 0.1075 is not unique and depends on the stereotomy exercised. Using vertical cuts associated with a Cartesian coordinate system yields a different, slightly higher value for the minimum thickness of t/R = 0.1095." — [Aston research portal, Makris & Alexakis](https://research.aston.ac.uk/en/publications/the-effect-of-stereotomy-on-the-shape-of-the-thrust-line-and-the-); Ochsendorf's value "t/R = 0.10748 and the position of the intrados hinge at θ = 54.484 degrees" and Heyman's "θ = 58.8 degrees ... t/R = 0.106" — [search summary of Gaspar et al. 2021, Scipedia](https://www.scipedia.com/public/Gaspar_et_al_2021a)
- Cocchetti, Colasante & Rizzi (Applied Mechanics Reviews, 2012) give the most precise values in the literature: critical thickness η_CCR = 0.107426 with hinge position 54.4833° (Milankovitch η_M = 0.107478), versus Heyman's η_H = 0.105965 — quoted in [Forgács et al. 2017](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/144597/) ("as described by Cocchetti et al."); the primary paper was read locally but its URL was not captured (see Gaps).
- **Iannuzzo (2017) rigid-block LP reproduction of Couplet's problem, normalised by INTRADOS radius R = 1 m:** analytical minimum thickness t/R = 0.1135818 (hinge angle 35.52° from the crown... table gives "0.00 0.1135818" for springing angle β = 0°), numerical bracket "[0.11357, 0.11359]"; β = 30°: analytical 0.0231130, numerical "[0.0230, 0.0233]"; β = 60°: analytical 0.0015265, numerical "[0.00151, 0.00155]". "The solution ... is practically coincident to the analytical one (Tab. 6.2) and corresponds exactly to that found by Ochsendorf (2006)." 500 elements for β = 0°, 100 for β = 30°. — [Iannuzzo PhD thesis, §6.5, pp. 151–155](https://www.fedoa.unina.it/11732/1/Iannuzzo_Antonino_29.pdf)
- **Iannuzzo (2017) circular arch under self-weight plus mass-proportional horizontal load λ (closed form vs rigid-block LP):** round arch (β = 0°) with "internal radius R = 1.00, and thickness s = 0.25", 80 rigid elements: numerical λ ∈ [0.334, 0.340], analytical λ = 0.340, hinge angles (analytical) 0.00°, 47.47°, 108.75°, 170.04°. Depressed arch β = 15° (embrace 150°), t = 0.15, 100 elements: numerical [0.390, 0.395], analytical 0.392, hinges 15.00°, 54.29°, 111.32°, 165.00°. β = 30°, t = 0.10, 120 elements: numerical [0.548, 0.549], analytical 0.549, hinges 30.00°, 63.59°, 114.97°, 150.00°. — [Iannuzzo PhD thesis, §6.6, pp. 156–160](https://www.fedoa.unina.it/11732/1/Iannuzzo_Antonino_29.pdf)
- Alexakis & Makris, "Limit equilibrium analysis and the minimum thickness of circular masonry arches to withstand lateral inertial loading", Archive of Applied Mechanics 84(5) (2014) 757–772, DOI 10.1007/s00419-014-0831-4: "vertical ruptures occur at lower accelerations than radial ruptures" and the multiplicity of minimum-uplift solutions "is a direct consequence of the multiple possible ways that a masonry arch with finite thickness may rupture"; findings "align well with distinct element software simulations". — [Aston research portal](https://research.aston.ac.uk/en/publications/limit-equilibrium-analysis-and-the-minimum-thickness-of-circular-)
- Cavalagli, Gusella & Severini (Perugia) extend Heyman's lateral-load analysis to pointed arches: "four types of collapse modes can be identified: no-springing, left-springing, right-springing and two-springing"; results are presented as curves of minimum horizontal load multiplier k against thickness for eccentricities e_p/R_c = 0.65 and 1.5 — [Perugia IRIS PDF](https://research.unipg.it/bitstream/11391/1386045/10/CGS_IJMS_R1_g.pdf)
- **Drucker's non-associativity result:** D.C. Drucker, "Coulomb friction, plasticity and limit loads", J. Applied Mechanics 21:71–74 (cited by Ferris & Tin-Loi as ref. [3], year printed as 1953 in their list) — [TR 99-01, p. 14](https://minds.wisconsin.edu/bitstream/handle/1793/64402/99-01.pdf). Secondary: "Drucker (1954) proved that the analogy between friction and plasticity is far from complete for limit loads due to the non-associative nature of Coulomb friction ... The limit load of a system with frictional interfaces is bounded from below by the limit load with zero friction, and from above by the limit load for no relative sliding and by the limit load for a cohesionless soil with dilatancy angle less than or equal to the friction angle." — [iMechanica note "Plasticity and friction"](https://www.imechanica.org/sites/default/files/plasticity_and_friction_imechanica.pdf)
- Two-block stack experiments exist for 3-D interface laws: Casapulla & Portioli tested "a stack of two dry-jointed tuff blocks subjected to different loading conditions implying interactions among shear, torsion and bending moments" and compared with an analytical two-rigid-block model (Construction and Building Materials 78 (2015) 126–143); related torsion–shear experiments in WIT Trans. Built Env. 153 — [WIT Press](https://witpress.com/elibrary/wit-transactions-on-the-built-environment/153/34277)
- Smallest published flow-rule-sensitive rigid-block cases found: Ferris & Tin-Loi Example 1, 33 blocks, LP 0.64286 vs MPEC 0.63898 (0.6 %) — [TR 99-01, Table 1](https://minds.wisconsin.edu/bitstream/handle/1793/64402/99-01.pdf); Orduña & Lourenço pile, ≤36 blocks, minimum 0.427 / maximum 0.553 / FEM 0.479 — [Part II](https://hdl.handle.net/1822/4600)

### Inferences
- Normalisation trap: Iannuzzo's 0.11358 and Milankovitch's 0.1075 are the same arch. 0.1075 is t divided by the centre-line radius; dividing by the intrados radius gives 0.1075/(1 − 0.1075/2) = 0.1136. A known-answer test must state which radius it uses.
- The Couplet–Heyman and lateral-λ arch problems are flow-rule independent (hinging only, no sliding at the quoted thicknesses), so they test equilibrium/moment handling without ambiguity. The 3DEC incline cases are also flow-rule independent at the single-block level (sliding criterion is tan ψ versus tan φ regardless of dilatancy).
- Every wall/pile problem with stepped-crack or sliding mechanisms (Ferris & Tin-Loi 2–6, Orduña C–E) is flow-rule sensitive; the associative LP is always the upper value, the "minimise α" MPEC the lower, and FEM/load-path answers fall in between. An engine must declare which convention it implements before any of these can be pass/fail.
- A practical minimal suite, in increasing complexity: (1) 3DEC incline regimes; (2) 3DEC wedge φ_crit = 33.36°; (3) semicircular arch t/R = 0.1075 (centre-line) / hinge 54.48°; (4) arch λ = 0.340 for R_i = 1, t = 0.25; (5) Ferris & Tin-Loi Example 1 (LP 0.64286); (6) Ferris & Tin-Loi Example 4 (LP 0.33195 / MPEC 0.26374) as the flow-rule discriminator; (7) Orduña pile bracket [0.427, 0.553].

### Gaps
- No source was found publishing load factors for a two-stacked-block, three-voussoir arch or four-block portal with both associative and non-associative friction; these appear to be textbook exercises rather than published benchmarks. Trentadue & Quaranta 2013 mention "arches, portals and panels" but the paper is paywalled.
- Cocchetti, Colasante & Rizzi's primary PDF was read from a local copy without a recoverable URL; the values are therefore cited through Forgács et al. 2017. The published reference is Applied Mechanics Reviews 64(5) (2012) 050802 (DOI not verified online in this session).
- Kao et al. 2022 tilting-table benchmark values (the modern three-cube and arch experiments) were not accessible.
