# Classical closed-form rigid-body statics benchmarks for a rigid-block structural engine

Scope: scenarios in which the stand/fall answer (and which block moves, and how) follows from closed-form statics or from a published experiment with reported numbers. Every scenario states its mechanical assumptions explicitly, because each benchmark is only a benchmark under those assumptions:

- **RB** — perfectly rigid blocks, point/edge contacts (infinite compressive strength, no crushing, no joint deformation).
- **NT** — zero tensile strength at joints (no cohesion); blocks can push but not pull.
- **NS** — sliding excluded (friction assumed sufficient); a separate sliding check is needed.
- **CF** — Coulomb friction, limit F ≤ μN, static = kinetic coefficient.
- **MT** — "middle-third"/linear-compression fracture model (Ochsendorf/Heyman) — a continuum with linear compressive stress distribution; the fracture forms where the resultant leaves the kern. Not a pure rigid-block result; a discrete-block engine will only reproduce it if the blocks are small enough relative to the fracture geometry.

All quantities SI. g = 9.81 m/s² where a numeric value is needed. Where a source is imperial or gram-force, the conversion is stated.

Working note on sources: several primary PDFs (Housner 1963, Ochsendorf 2002 thesis, Paterson–Zwick, Prieto/Peña 2006, RAEng, MIT OCW lecture) were text-extracted locally with `pdftotext`; equations in the thesis were OCR-garbled, so each buttress equation below was re-derived from the stated free-body diagrams and **checked numerically against the thesis's own worked example and model tests** (agreement to 3 significant figures, shown inline). Those re-derivations are marked "(reconstructed; verified against source numbers)".

## 1. Block on an inclined plane — sliding vs tipping threshold

### Takeaway
For a uniform rectangular block of base width w and height h on a plane tilted to angle θ, sliding impends at tan θ_s = μ and tipping impends at tan θ_t = w/h; the lower angle governs, so the block tips first iff μ > w/h. Both thresholds are exact rigid-body results (RB, CF). No peer-reviewed experiment was found that reports the *crossover* itself, but each half is confirmed separately: tilt tests are the standard way to measure μ (tan θ_s), and 1:5 tilt-table tests on dry-stone walls confirm tan θ_t = thickness/height for simple overturning (see §4).

### Cited Findings
- Royal Academy of Engineering teaching resource "Friction and sliding" derives the sliding angle: resolving normal and along the plane gives F = mg sin θ, R = mg cos θ, limit F = μR, hence "tan θ = μ … the object slides when the tangent of the angle of the inclined plane is equal to the coefficient of friction"; worked value μ = 0.7 → θ = 34.99°. It then states the tipping condition "an object of uniform density on an inclined plane will tip when the angle of the incline θ satisfies tan θ = w/h, where w is the object width and h is the object height", and the decision rule: "If μ < w/h then θ_s < θ_t … the object will slide before tipping over … If μ > w/h then θ_s > θ_t … the object will tip over before sliding … The object will remain stationary when the angle of incline satisfies both θ < θ_s and θ < θ_t." — [RAEng, Friction and sliding (PDF)](https://raeng.org.uk/media/vuxfappb/6-friction-and-sliding.pdf)
- Same resource notes what happens after a tip: "After toppling the object will have taken a 90° rotation which will switch the height and width dimensions, resulting in a new topple angle θ_t … The new configuration may have a value of θ_t that is smaller than the previous one. In this case the toppling will continue." (useful as a multi-stage expectation) — [RAEng](https://raeng.org.uk/media/vuxfappb/6-friction-and-sliding.pdf)
- Baker & Haynes, *Engineering Statics: Open and Interactive*, §9.2 "Slipping vs. Tipping": the slip limit is F_max = μ_s N; "The object will tip when the resultant normal force N shifts off the end of the object, because it no longer acts on the object so it can't contribute to equilibrium"; "If P exceeds the smaller of the limiting values, it will initiate the corresponding impending motion." At tipping, friction is static but not impending. — [Baker & Haynes, Ch. 9 slipping vs tipping](https://engineeringstatics.org/Chapter_09-slipping-vs--tipping.html)
- Standard problem instance (JEE-style, widely reproduced): "A block of base 10 cm × 10 cm and height 15 cm is kept on an inclined plane. The coefficient of friction between them is √3 …" — the tipping angle is arctan(10/15) = 33.7° and sliding would need arctan(√3) = 60°, so the block topples. (Problem statement retrieved only from search listing; the solution page returned 404.) — [getmarks listing](https://getmarks.app/questions/a-block-of-base-10-cm-10-cm-and-height-15-cm-is-kept-on-an-inclined-plane-the-coefficient-of-friction-between-them-is-3-the-inclination-of-this-inclin)
- Experimental confirmation of the sliding half: tilt tests are the standard laboratory method for the basic friction angle of rock joints; Alejano, González & Muralha (2012) compared tilt-testing techniques and found "coefficients of variation for the basic friction angle in the range of 5–10 %", and that cylinder-generatrix tests are not appropriate for planar surfaces. — [Alejano et al. 2012, Rock Mech. Rock Eng., DOI 10.1007/s00603-012-0265-7](https://repositorio.lnec.pt/handle/123456789/1003995)
- Experimental confirmation of the tipping half: Restrepo-Vélez & Magenes tilted 1:5 dry-stone (marble-block) wall specimens on a tilting platform; "The collapse multiplier was calculated from the inclination angle of the platform", shear along joints "purely by friction", 42 configurations. — [Restrepo Vélez & Magenes 2009, Rose Report 2009/02, ISBN 978-88-6198-042-6](https://bookstore.eucentre.it/?p=662); journal version [Restrepo Vélez, Magenes & Griffith, Int. J. Archit. Heritage, DOI 10.1080/15583058.2012.663059](https://iris.unipv.it/handle/11571/461976)

### Inferences
- **Exact equivalence tilt ↔ horizontal load.** Tilting the base by θ is exactly equivalent (for rigid blocks) to a horizontal body force λW with λ = tan θ at full weight: both thresholds depend only on the ratio of tangential to normal gravity components. So every tilt-table result doubles as a "mass-proportional horizontal load" result with collapse multiplier λ₀ = tan θ₀.
- **Worked instance (SI).** Block 0.10 m wide × 0.15 m tall × 0.10 m deep, concrete ρ = 2400 kg/m³ → m = 3.60 kg, W = 35.3 N. θ_t = arctan(0.10/0.15) = **33.69°**. With μ = 0.70, θ_s = arctan 0.70 = **34.99°** → tips first at 33.69° (margin 1.3°). With μ = 0.60, θ_s = **30.96°** → slides first. With μ = 0.667 (= w/h) the two coincide — a good "degenerate" test where either mode is acceptable but motion must start at 33.69°.
- **Which block/body moves:** single block; assert mode (rotation about the downhill bottom edge vs translation along the plane) and onset angle. Displacement magnitude is not a valid assertion; mode + onset angle are.
- **Sensitivity.** dθ/dμ = 1/(1+μ²): at μ = 0.7 a ±0.01 change in μ moves θ_s by ±0.38°. dθ_t/d(w/h) = 1/(1+(w/h)²): at w/h = 2/3 a 1 % error in w or h moves θ_t by ±0.26°. The thresholds are far apart enough that a 0.5° tolerance distinguishes the modes in the instances above. Note that a finite contact patch or compliant contact in the engine moves the pivot inward from the edge and lowers θ_t; the Peña data in §2 show the same effect on real granite (measured α below theoretical).
- **Assumptions:** RB, CF. No NT needed (no joints). If the engine models the block–plane contact with cohesion c or a tensile limit, both thresholds rise; the benchmark requires c = 0, tensile limit = 0.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed paper was found reporting an experiment that scans μ or w/h across the crossover and measures which mode occurs; the crossover rule is textbook statics. The RAEng "static at 34°, sliding at 35°" check is from its own interactive simulation, not a physical test.

## 2. Housner's rocking block (1963): static threshold and experimental confirmation

### Takeaway
For a rigid rectangular block of half-width b and half-height h on a rigid base (no sliding), rocking initiates when the horizontal base acceleration satisfies a/g ≥ tan α = b/h (Housner wrote the small-angle form a/g > α). This static-uplift condition is exact and uncontested; Housner's *dynamic* overturning formulas for finite pulses are known to be wrong and must not be used as benchmarks. Quasi-static confirmation comes from free-rocking fits on granite blocks (Peña/Prieto/Lourenço: measured α within 3 % of b/h for h/b = 4 and 6) and from tilt-table overturning of walls (§4). Housner's own paper contains no experiments.

### Cited Findings
- Citation: Housner, G. W. (1963). "The behavior of inverted pendulum structures during earthquakes." *Bulletin of the Seismological Society of America*, 53(2), 403–417. DOI 10.1785/BSSA0530020403. — [Caltech Authors record](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/47803)
- Geometry and free rocking (p. 403–404): "It is assumed that the coefficient of friction is sufficiently large so that there will be no sliding between the block and the base. The significant properties of the block are its weight, W, its moment of inertia, I₀, about the point O, and the location of the center of gravity a distance h above the base and a distance b from the side of the block … the radial distance from the center of rotation O to the center of gravity is R = √(h² + b²). When the block is at rest, the line R makes an angle α with the vertical." Equation of motion: "I₀ d²θ/dt² = −WR sin(α − θ)" (eq. 1); for α < about 20° the small-angle form "I₀ θ̈ − WRθ = −WRα" with p² = WR/I₀. — [Housner 1963 (PDF via Caltech)](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/47803)
- Static threshold (p. 407–408, "Overturning by constant acceleration"): "For small angles of oscillation the effect of the acceleration is the same as if a force Wa/g were acting horizontally through the center of gravity of the block and thus the equation of motion is: I₀θ̈ − WRθ = WRa/g − WRα (7). It is seen from this equation that a necessary condition for motion to be initiated is that a/g > α, which specifies the fraction of g acceleration required to begin tilting the block." — [Housner 1963](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/47803)
- Exact (non-small-angle) form of the same criterion, as used in the modern literature: "To trigger rocking motion, the overturning moment due to inertial action should exceed the restoring moment due to gravity, which can be written as ü_g/g ≥ tan α; for slenderness angles α of less than approximately 20 degrees (Yim et al 1980), the trigonometric argument can be dropped." Block of dimensions 2b × 2h, "R = (b²+h²)^½ and a dimensionless slenderness angle α = tan⁻¹(b/h). The interface between the block and the rigid base is both tensionless and … assumed to be sufficiently large to prevent sliding during rocking." For rectangular geometry I₀ = (4/3)mR². — [Voyagaki, Psycharis & Mylonakis, 15WCEE 2012 paper 0353](https://www.iitk.ac.in/nicee/wcee/article/WCEE2012_0353.pdf)
- Restitution (energy loss per impact), as restated by Prieto, Peña, Lourenço & Lemos: for a rectangular block "α = tan⁻¹(b/h); R = √(b²+h²)" and "p = √(3g/4R); μ = 1 − (3/2) sin²(α)" where μ is the angular-velocity ratio across impact (Housner's r is its square, a kinetic-energy ratio). — [Prieto, Peña, Lourenço & Lemos, SAHC 2006 (PDF)](https://repositorium.uminho.pt/server/api/core/bitstreams/82d0aa07-bc14-4ce2-8463-83630b179900/content)
- Housner's finite-pulse overturning formulas are **incorrect** and should not be benchmarks: "the solution presented by Housner (1963) for the minimum acceleration amplitude of a half-sine pulse that is needed to overturn a rigid block is incorrect. In reality, under a half-sine pulse, a block overturns during its free vibration regime and not at the instant that the pulse expires." — [Makris & Roussos, PEER Report 1998-05](https://peer.berkeley.edu/publications/1998-05). Charalampakis, Tsiatas & Tsopelas (2021) add that Housner's rectangular-pulse solution of the equation of motion was also wrong, "which led to an unrealistic overturning condition for the rectangular pulse". — [arXiv:2106.11741](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.11741)
- Scale effect (dynamic, not static): "comparing two blocks of the same proportions, the larger block is more stable than the smaller. There is thus an unexpected scale effect which is explained by the fact that the ground motion is not scaled with the block." — [Housner 1963](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/47803)
- **Experiment (quasi-static parameters).** Four blue-granite blocks tested on the LNEC shaking table; "The dimensions of the single specimens 1, 2 and 3 were fixed to achieve a Height–Width ratio (h/b) of 4, 6 and 8, respectively"; specimen 4 had "a large 45 degree cut (40 mm) at the base". Table 1 (as extracted; width 2b, height 2h, thickness 2t, mass): #1 0.25 × 1.000 × 0.754 m, 503 kg; #2 0.17 × 1.000 × 0.502 m, 228 kg; #3 0.12 × 1.000 × 0.375 m, 120 kg; #4 0.16 × 0.457 × 0.750 m, 245 kg; base block 1.00 × 0.250 × 0.750 m, 500 kg. Table 2, theoretical vs experimental α (rad): #1 0.242 / 0.235; #2 0.168 / 0.163; #3 0.119 / 0.154; #4 0.310 / 0.268. "Experimental parameters for specimens 1 and 2 are similar to the theoretical values, with differences smaller than 3%." Explanation offered: "the rotation points are not located at the corners since the material is not perfectly rigid", leading to an equivalent half-base b_eq = h tan(α_exp). Friction angle used in the DEM model: 30°. — [Prieto, Peña, Lourenço & Lemos, SAHC 2006](https://repositorium.uminho.pt/server/api/core/bitstreams/82d0aa07-bc14-4ce2-8463-83630b179900/content). Full journal version: Peña, Prieto, Lourenço, Campos Costa & Lemos (2007), "On the dynamics of rocking motion of single rigid-block structures", *Earthquake Eng. Struct. Dyn.* 36(17), 2383–2399, 275 shaking-table tests. — [DOI 10.1002/eqe.739](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/eqe.739)
- Other rocking experiments referenced for completeness (dynamic, not static thresholds): Lipscombe & Pellegrino (1993) "Free rocking of prismatic blocks", *J. Eng. Mech.* 119(7), 1387–1410 — "the standard mathematical model for this problem is often inaccurate … free-rocking response of short blocks depends crucially on bouncing after each impact". — [ASCE DOI 10.1061/(ASCE)0733-9399(1993)119:7(1387)](https://ascelibrary.com/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%290733-9399%281993%29119%3A7%281387%29). ElGawady et al. (2011): ten blocks, aspect ratios 0.7–5, on timber/steel/concrete/rubber bases; models "generally found to overestimate the rotation amplitudes and periods". — [Univ. Auckland record](https://researchspace.auckland.ac.nz/handle/2292/25718?show=full)

### Inferences
- **Benchmark statement.** Block 2b × 2h × depth d, uniform density, on a rigid frictional base with μ ≥ b/h (so sliding is excluded — see §4 for the coupled case). Under a horizontal body acceleration a (or equivalently a base tilt θ with tan θ = a/g): no motion for a/g < b/h; rotation about the downhill bottom edge for a/g > b/h. This is exact: moment of the inertial force m·a about the toe is m a h, restoring moment m g b.
- **Worked instance (specimen #1 above):** 2b = 0.25 m, 2h = 1.00 m, d = 0.754 m, m = 503 kg (ρ ≈ 2670 kg/m³). b/h = 0.25 → α = 14.04°, threshold a = 0.25 g = **2.45 m/s²**, or base tilt **14.04°**. R = 0.515 m; p = √(3g/4R) = 3.78 s⁻¹ (matches the paper's theoretical 3.78). Specimen #3 (2b = 0.12 m): threshold a = 0.12 g = **1.18 m/s²**, tilt **6.84°**; but note the measured α for #3 was 0.154 rad (8.8°), i.e. the real block behaved as if *wider* — the paper attributes this to non-rigid contact, so an engine with ideal edge contact should match the 6.84° theoretical value, not the measured one.
- **Assumptions:** RB, NT at the base, NS. Infinite compressive strength is essential: finite strength moves the pivot inward and lowers the threshold (as the granite data hint).
- **Sensitivity.** Threshold is linear in b/h; a 1 % geometry error gives a 1 % threshold error; the angle α changes by 0.14° per 1 % at b/h = 0.25. Housner's small-angle α vs exact tan α differ by 1.6 % at α = 14° (0.2450 vs 0.2500) — assert against tan α = b/h, not α.
- **Housner's "scale model experiments"** do not exist in the 1963 paper; it cites Milne (1881/1893) and Kirkpatrick (1927) for earlier column-overthrow work. Confirmation of the static threshold is therefore secondary (Peña et al.; tilt tables in §4).

### Gaps
- The journal paper's (Peña et al. 2007) full specimen table could not be opened (publisher blocked); the SAHC 2006 conference version's Table 1 was OCR-garbled and the row→specimen assignment above is the most consistent reading (masses check against granite density for #1–#3 but the 245 kg listed for #4 does not match 0.16 × 0.457 × 0.75 m ≈ 145 kg at 2650 kg/m³; treat #4 with caution).
- Lipscombe & Pellegrino's block dimensions and ElGawady's exact data were not retrievable (paywalled abstracts only).

## 3. Stacked blocks: harmonic overhang, counterbalanced stacks, and uniform-offset stacks

### Takeaway
With one block per level, n identical frictionless blocks of length L can overhang at most (L/2)·H_n (H_n = Σ1/i), achieved when the centre of mass of the top k blocks sits exactly over the edge of block k+1 for every k — every interface is simultaneously critical. Allowing counterweights, 3 blocks reach overhang 1·L and 4 blocks (15 − 4√2)/8 · L ≈ 1.1679 L; the inverted 3-triangle and the 5-diamond are provably *unbalanced*, giving exact "this falls, that stands" cases. A uniform-offset (leaning) stack of n blocks with constant step d stands iff d ≤ L/n (derived here).

### Cited Findings
- Paterson, M. & Zwick, U., "Overhang" (arXiv 0710.2357; *Amer. Math. Monthly* 116(1), 2009): "How far off the edge of the table can we reach by stacking n identical, homogeneous, frictionless blocks of length 1? A classical solution achieves an overhang asymptotic to ½ ln n … The classical harmonic stack of size n is composed of n blocks stacked one on top of the other, with the ith block from the top extending by 1/(2i) beyond the block below it … The overhang achieved by the construction is clearly ½H_n … The harmonic stack of size n is balanced since, for every i < n, the center of mass of the topmost i blocks lies exactly above the right-hand edge of the (i+1)st block … Similarly, the center of mass of all the n blocks lies exactly above the right edge of the table." — [arXiv:0710.2357](https://arxiv.org/abs/0710.2357)
- Same paper, the formal balance model used (relevant to how an engine should check equilibrium): "We assume that edges of all the blocks are completely smooth, so that there is no friction between them. All the forces exerted on block A by block B … are therefore vertical … blocks A and B can push, but not pull, one another." "Definition 2.1 (Balance). A stack of blocks is balanced if and only if it admits a balancing set of forces." "Theorem 2.2. The balance of a stack containing n blocks can be decided by checking the feasibility of a collection of linear equations and inequalities with O(n) variables and constraints." Also: "Inverted triangles show that simple center-of-mass considerations are not enough to determine the balance of stacks." — [arXiv:0710.2357](https://arxiv.org/abs/0710.2357)
- Same paper, small-n optimal (multiwide) overhangs in block lengths: n=2: 0.75; 3: 1; 4: 1.16789 ("Ainley [1] found the maximum overhang achievable with four blocks to be (15 − 4√2)/8 ≈ 1.16789"); 5: 1.30455; 6: 1.4367; 7: 1.53005; 8: 1.63151; 9: 1.71527; 10: 1.78713; … 19: 2.27713; 20: 2.32014; 30: 2.70909 (numerical optima, "no formal optimality proofs" for n ≥ 5). "Given the fact that the 3-block stack that achieves the maximum overhang is an inverted 2-triangle, it is natural to enquire whether larger inverted triangles are also balanced. Unfortunately, the next inverted triangle is already unbalanced." "The m-diamond uses m² blocks and would give an overhang of m/2, but unfortunately it is unbalanced for m ≥ 5"; "The balanced 4-diamond and the unbalanced 5-diamond" (Fig. 8). Parabolic stacks achieve overhang ∝ n^{1/3} ("111 blocks … overhang of 3"). — [arXiv:0710.2357](https://arxiv.org/abs/0710.2357)
- Harmonic values (single-wide), in block lengths: n=1: 1/2; 2: 3/4; 3: 11/12 ≈ 0.9167; 4: 25/24 ≈ 1.0417; 5: 137/120 ≈ 1.1417; 6: 49/40 = 1.225; 7: 363/280 ≈ 1.2964; 8: 761/560 ≈ 1.3589; 9: 7129/5040 ≈ 1.4145; 10: 7381/5040 ≈ 1.4645. "For three blocks, counterbalanced stacking achieves an overhang of 1, compared to the single-wide maximum of 11/12." History: Johnson, P. B. (1955) "Leaning Tower of Lire", *Am. J. Phys.* 23, 240; Hall (2005) on robustness to non-ideal conditions. — [Wikipedia, Block-stacking problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Block-stacking_problem)
- "The balancing condition is that the vertical projection of the center of mass of the books above each interface lie over the supporting region at that interface"; d_n = ½H_n; books needed for overhang 1, 2, 3 book-lengths: 4, 31, 227. References include Gamow & Stern (1958) *Puzzle-Math*; Gardner (1971); Graham, Knuth & Patashnik (1990). — [MathWorld, Book Stacking Problem](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/BookStackingProblem.html)
- Paterson–Zwick note the harmonic stack appears in mechanics textbooks since the mid-19th century, and that the inverted triangles "are falsely claimed by Jargodzki and Potter [11] (Challenge 271 …) to be balanced" — a caution that even published sources get the balance of multiwide stacks wrong. — [arXiv:0710.2357](https://arxiv.org/abs/0710.2357)

### Inferences
- **Benchmark A — harmonic stack (exact, knife-edge).** n blocks, length L = 0.20 m, height 0.05 m, depth 0.10 m, ρ = 600 kg/m³ (wood) → m = 0.60 kg each. Offsets from the top: L/2, L/4, L/6, … For n = 4: overhang of top block beyond the table edge = (25/24)(0.20)/2·2 = 0.2083 m, i.e. **0.208 m**. Every interface is exactly critical, so the configuration is marginal: assert that offsets scaled by (1 − δ) stand and by (1 + δ) collapse for δ ≈ 10⁻³–10⁻². Under uniform (1 + δ) scaling all interfaces are violated at once, so "which block moves first" is ill-posed; instead perturb a single block k by +δL and assert that blocks 1..k rotate about the edge of block k+1 while k+1..n stay. The contact-force check is purely vertical (frictionless suffices) — a good test of the engine's unilateral (push-only) contact without friction entering.
- **Benchmark B — counterbalanced 3-block (exact).** Two blocks side by side on top of one bottom block … (the inverted 2-triangle: one block on the table, two on it, overhang = 1 L): stands. The inverted 3-triangle (1 + 2 + 3 rows) **collapses**; the 4-diamond (1,2,3,4,3,2,1 rows, 16 blocks) stands; the 5-diamond (25 blocks) collapses. These are exact linear-feasibility results and test exactly the engine's redistribution across multi-support contacts, where naive centre-of-mass checks fail (per the paper's warning).
- **Benchmark C — uniform-offset ("leaning") stack (derived here; standard CoM argument).** n blocks of length L each shifted by a constant d relative to the one below. Numbering from the top, the centre of mass of the top k blocks lies (k − 1)d/2 inboard of the top block's centre; it must not pass the edge of block k+1, giving d ≤ L/(k+1) for k = 1..n−1. The binding case is k = n − 1 at the bottom interface: **stands iff d ≤ L/n**, and the first motion when d is slightly larger is rotation of the top n−1 blocks about the outer top edge of the bottom block (the ground is flat, so it imposes no constraint). Instance: L = 0.20 m, n = 5 → d_max = 0.040 m; total lean of the top block = (n−1)d = 0.16 m. With d = 0.041 m the top four blocks rotate as a body about the bottom block's edge; the bottom block does not move. (Assumptions: RB, NT, frictionless or any μ — no shear is needed in the limit state; it is a pure moment criterion.)
- **Sensitivity.** Harmonic: interface k is critical at overhang increment exactly L/(2k); a 1 % offset error at the top block (±1 mm on 100 mm) flips the top interface only. Offset stack: the margin at interface k is L/(k+1) − d, so near d = L/n the lower interfaces are marginal and upper ones have slack of order L/k − L/n.

### Gaps
- No published *physical* measurements of a harmonic stack with reported numbers were found (demonstrations are ubiquitous but anecdotal). Hall (2005), *Am. J. Phys.* 73, 1107, reportedly treats robustness to non-ideal blocks but was not retrieved.
- Johnson (1955) was not retrieved directly (cited via Wikipedia/MathWorld).

## 4. Free-standing rectangular wall or pier under horizontal load / tilted base: rocking vs sliding, collapse multiplier

### Takeaway
For a monolithic rigid wall of thickness t (B) and height H under a mass-proportional horizontal load λW (or a base tilt θ with λ = tan θ): overturning about the toe at λ_r = t/H; sliding at λ_s = μ; the smaller governs (RB, NT, CF). For a concentrated horizontal force P at height y: P_tip = W·t/(2y), P_slide = μW. Under *dynamic* base acceleration Shenton (1996) showed pure rocking needs μ larger than b/h by an acceleration-dependent amount (a slide–rock mode exists in between); the closed form for that boundary is derived below and is consistent with Shenton's description. 1:5 tilt-table tests on dry-stone marble walls (Restrepo-Vélez & Magenes) are the published experimental check of λ = tan θ against analytical collapse multipliers.

### Cited Findings
- Shenton, H. W. (1996). "Criteria for initiation of slide, rock, and slide-rock rigid-body modes." *J. Eng. Mech.* 122(7), 690–693. DOI 10.1061/(ASCE)0733-9399(1996)122:7(690). A rectangular block on an accelerating base can "rest, slide, rock, slide-rock, [or] free flight"; "a slide-rock mode can also be initiated from rest"; "it is incorrect to assume a rock mode simply occurs when static friction exceeds the width-to-height ratio; instead, a slide-rock mode initiates when friction is greater than this ratio but less than a critical value that depends on ground acceleration"; transitions "from sliding to slide-rock to pure rocking as friction increases for a given foundation acceleration"; restricted to rectangular blocks, Coulomb friction, horizontal acceleration. — [Shenton 1996 summary](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W2074878678); citation confirmed at [KoreaScience (Aydin 2006 ref list)](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO200628351321769.do)
- Housner's static criterion and the no-sliding assumption (base "both tensionless and … sufficiently large [friction] to prevent sliding"): see §2. — [Voyagaki et al. 2012](https://www.iitk.ac.in/nicee/wcee/article/WCEE2012_0353.pdf)
- Tilt-table verification programme: "static 1:5-scale tests on dry-stack stone masonry walls" to verify "analytical expressions for the computation of the static collapse multiplier of the out-of-plane failure mechanisms"; "The collapse multiplier was calculated from the inclination angle of the platform that caused collapse"; "Marble blocks" chosen for a realistic friction coefficient; mortar-free so "shear strength along the joints was given purely by friction"; 42 configurations varying length, openings, staggering ratio, wall connections, overburden, storeys. — [Restrepo Vélez, Magenes & Griffith 2013/14, IJAH, DOI 10.1080/15583058.2012.663059](https://iris.unipv.it/handle/11571/461976); [Rose Report 2009/02](https://bookstore.eucentre.it/?p=662)
- Masonry buttress/tower under lateral load: "At their limit state, tall, slender masonry buttresses and towers collapse by pivoting about their base corner, whereas less slender masonry structures may collapse by developing a shear failure." — [Makris & Alexakis 2015, Arch. Appl. Mech. 85, 1915–1940, DOI 10.1007/s00419-015-1027-2](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2015/478)
- Sliding check used by Ochsendorf for a masonry pier (typical μ): "Typical values of f_s are 0.5–0.7, for stone on stone (Rankine 1858)." — [Ochsendorf 2002 thesis, §3.5](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/handle/1810/244820)
- Case-study instance of a sliding vs overturning comparison (Old Goa church buttress, 1 m strip, γ = 25 kN/m³): "the weight of the buttress above the springing is 61 kN and the weight of the arch provides a vertical force of 64 kN. Assuming a static coefficient of friction of 0.7, sliding will occur for horizontal forces greater than 88 kN. Therefore the buttress will fail by overturning before sliding" (overturning capacity 69 kN fractured / 112 kN solid). — [MIT OCW 4.448 Lecture 4 (Ochsendorf)](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/4-448-analysis-of-historic-structures-fall-2004/aa1e9e8eb38dc698f33c66d1dbe205c2_lec4_masonry3.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Benchmark D — monolithic wall, mass-proportional load / tilt (exact).** Wall t = 0.20 m, H = 2.00 m, length 1.00 m, ρ = 2000 kg/m³ → m = 800 kg, W = 7848 N. λ_r = t/H = **0.100** (tilt 5.71°). With μ = 0.6, λ_s = 0.6 ≫ 0.1 → rocks. With μ = 0.08 (e.g. a greased base) → slides at λ = 0.08 before rocking. Crossover at μ = 0.10. Assert: mode and onset λ. Equivalent concentrated-force form: P at mid-height y = 1.0 m → P_tip = W t/(2y) = 7848 × 0.20/2.0 = **785 N**; P_slide = μW = 4709 N (μ = 0.6).
- **Benchmark E — coursed wall (same wall built from n courses with NT, CF joints).** With rigid courses the overturning multiplier is still t/H about the base toe (the worst interface is the lowest, since the centroid height of the part above any joint at height z is (H − z)/2 and its half-width is still t/2 → λ at joint z is t/(H − z) ≥ t/H). So the mechanism must be a single hinge at the base; a correct engine must *not* open a higher joint first. Sliding is likewise worst at the base only if μ is uniform (shear/normal = λ at every joint under mass-proportional load, so all joints reach μ simultaneously — a degenerate case; use a base μ slightly below the joint μ to make the base slide first, or vice versa).
- **Benchmark F — dynamic mode boundaries (derived; consistent with Shenton's qualitative result).** Block half-width b, half-height h, β = b/h, base acceleration a, ā = a/g, I_O = (4/3)mR². From rest: no motion if ā < min(μ, β). Slide (no rock) if μ < β and ā > μ. For ā > β, rotation about the toe starts with θ̈ = (3/(4R²))(a h − g b); the friction required to supply the CG's horizontal acceleration is F = m(a − θ̈h) against N = m(g + θ̈b), so pure rock needs
  μ ≥ μ_cr(ā, β) = [ā(1 + 4β²) + 3β] / [4 + β² + 3βā],
  which equals β at ā = β and grows with ā; for β < μ < μ_cr the block slides and rocks together. Instance: β = 0.25, ā = 0.5 → μ_cr = (0.5·1.25 + 0.75)/(4 + 0.0625 + 0.375) = 1.375/4.4375 = **0.310**; so with μ = 0.28 a block that would rock under quasi-static loading enters slide-rock under a 0.5 g step. This boundary is a *dynamic* statement (depends on inertia), relevant only if the engine integrates dynamics; the quasi-static engine benchmark is the min(μ, β) rule.
- **Assumptions:** RB, NT, CF, infinite compressive strength. If the engine gives joints cohesion c, add c·A to the sliding resistance: P_slide = μN + cA — benchmark with c = 0 first.
- **Sensitivity:** λ_r is linear in t and 1/H; a 5 mm error on t = 0.20 m shifts λ_r by 2.5 %. Where μ ≈ t/H the mode is decided by second-order effects (contact compliance, pivot location) — avoid near-coincident instances for mode assertions.

### Gaps
- Shenton's printed closed-form expressions were not accessible (abstract-only); the μ_cr formula above is my derivation from the stated free-body conditions and matches his described limits (equals b/h at onset, rises with acceleration), but has not been checked character-by-character against the paper.
- Restrepo-Vélez & Magenes's per-specimen tilt angles and the measured-vs-analytical ratios were not retrievable (report body paywalled; the IJAH abstract gives only the programme). The analytic simple-overturning expression they verify, for a free one-leaf wall with no overburden, is λ₀ = t/h as derived above.

## 5. Ochsendorf, Hernando & Huerta (2004) and Ochsendorf (2002): collapse of masonry buttresses, fracture, leaning, and scale-model tests

### Takeaway
A masonry buttress of width b, height h_b, depth-density γ (weight per unit elevation area), carrying a vertical load V at its loaded edge and a horizontal thrust H at height h, overturns monolithically at H_s = (b/h)(W_b/2 + V). Treating the masonry as a no-tension continuum with a linear compressive stress distribution, a straight fracture forms from the loaded-side base corner up to height e = ξh on the pivot face, shedding a triangular wedge, and the capacity drops to H_u, typically 20–30 % below H_s. ξ solves a quadratic that is independent of b. Plywood-block scale models (0.728 m tall, 0.182 m wide) collapsed within ~6 % of the fractured prediction, 6–25 % below the monolithic value. Leaning by a small angle φ (radians) reduces capacity linearly, ≈ φ (W_b/2 + V)(h_b/h). All of this assumes infinite compressive strength, zero tensile strength and no sliding (MT + NT + NS).

### Cited Findings
- Journal paper: Ochsendorf, J. A., Hernando, J. I. & Huerta, S. (2004). "Collapse of masonry buttresses." *J. Architectural Engineering* (ASCE) 10(3), 88–97. DOI 10.1061/(ASCE)1076-0431(2004)10:3(88). Abstract: "A fracture forms at the collapse state, significantly decreasing the resistance to overturning. Conventional analysis assumes that a masonry buttress acts monolithically to resist lateral loads. The current paper demonstrates that this approach is clearly unsafe … By treating the masonry as a continuum, infinitely strong in compression, with no resistance to tension and no possibility for sliding, the writers demonstrate the form of the fracture and determine the critical failure load for typical buttress forms." — [Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1061/(ASCE)1076-0431(2004)10:3(88)?fields=title,authors,year,venue,abstract,externalIds)

- Mechanism (thesis §3.2–3.3): "a buttress is statically determinate to begin with and needs only one hinge to collapse"; "As a consequence of the unilateral character of the material … some fracture of the buttress can be expected at collapse, similar to leaning towers or walls of masonry (Heyman 1992). - Equilibrium equations (thesis §3.4, OCR-recovered and re-derived): stabilising moment about the pivot O (outer base corner) M_s = W_b x_b + V b [3.9]; detached wedge weight W_c = b e γ/2 [3.11]; overturning: H = (M_s − b² e γ/3)/h [3.12]; wedge equilibrium (resultant from above at the third-point b/3): (W_b − W_c + V)(b/3) − H e = 0 [3.13] → H = b(W_b + V)/(3e) − b²γ/6 [3.14]. - Rectangular buttress, dimensionless form (thesis §3.6): ξ = e/h, μ = h/h_b, ψ = V/W_b; "the critical value of ξ is independent of the scale of the buttress. Remarkably, the fracture location does not depend on the width, b … for the case of the vertical force equal to one half the weight of the buttress (ψ = 0.5) the fracture height will occur at ξ = 0.5 for any value of μ … For typical values of applied vertical load (ψ = 0 to 0.5), the fracture height will vary between ξ = 0.7 to 0.5." Monolithic capacity [3.26]: H_s = (b²γ/μ)(1/2 + ψ). "The horizontal load capacity of typical buttresses is 20%–30% less than the capacity of a monolithic buttress." — [thesis](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/bitstream/1810/244820/1/Ochsendorf25710.pdf)
- Thesis worked example (§3.6): "a rectangular buttress with a height h_b of 12 m and a base width b of 3 m … horizontal force is applied at a height h of 8 m, so that μ = 0.67.   - Overturning with wedge: **H = (b W_b/h)(1/2 + ψ − ξμ/3)** [3.23].
  - Wedge/fracture condition: **H = (b W_b/h)[(1 + ψ)/(3ξ) − μ/6]** [3.24].
  - Eliminating H: **μ ξ² − (3/2 + 3ψ + μ/2) ξ + (1 + ψ) = 0**, root in (0,1) [3.25]. Check: ψ = 0.5 → ξ = 0.5 for all μ (as stated in the thesis). Thesis example (μ = 0.667, ψ = 0.0943): ξ = 0.651, e = 5.21 m, H_u = (3·1060/8)(0.5943 − 0.1447) = 178.7 kN — matches 0.65 / 5.2 m / 178 kN. Sliding: 0.7 × ((12 − 8)·3·29.4 + 100) = 317 kN ✓.
  - Geometry of the wedge: the detached triangle has vertices at the loaded-face base corner, the loaded face at height e, and the pivot face at height e (its hypotenuse is the fracture, which rises from the loaded-side base corner to the pivot face at height e); the attached triangle (pivot corner, loaded-side base corner, pivot face at e) stays. Its centroid is at 2b/3 from the pivot, which is where the b²eγ/3 term comes from.
  - Leaning (small φ, rad): **H_φ ≈ H_u − φ (W_b/2 + V)(h_b/h)**, so **φ_max ≈ H_u h / [(W_b/2 + V) h_b]**. Check against Goa: (452.5 + 64)(15.2/12.5)/57.3 = 11 kN per degree vs the lecture's "approximately 10 kN per degree"; the 0.4° case: 69 − 0.4·10 ≈ 65 kN ✓.
- **Benchmark G — full SI instance (thesis example).** Buttress b = 3.0 m, h_b = 12.0 m, depth 1.5 m, ρ = 2000 kg/m³ (W_b = 1.060 MN), V = 100 kN at the loaded top edge, H applied at h = 8.0 m, μ_friction = 0.7. Expected: monolithic rigid-block answer H_s = 234 kN (a pure rigid-block engine with one monolithic block will give this); no-tension continuum answer H_u = 178 kN with fracture height e = 5.2 m (a discrete-block engine will approach this only as block size → 0 and only if blocks are released when not held in compression); sliding at 317 kN (not governing). An engine built from, say, 0.5 m courses will land between 178 and 234 kN; the *test* should assert that the capacity is below H_s and that the mechanism is a base-hinge overturning with a lower-region wedge left behind, not sliding.
- **Benchmark H — the scale model (exact rigid-course version).** Rectangular model: b = 0.182 m, h_b = 0.728 m (h_b/b = 4), load at h = 0.364 m (μ = 0.5), plywood 27 mm courses with sand-glued faces (μ_s "typical of stone on stone", i.e. ≈ 0.5–0.7). From H_s = 0.25 W_b = 878 gf → **W_b = 3.51 kg** (34.4 N). Predictions reproduce exactly from the quadratic: ψ = 0 → ξ = 0.719, H_u = 0.190 W_b = 667 gf = **6.54 N**; measured **6.96 N** (710 gf); H_s = **8.61 N**. ψ = 0.142 (V = 500 gf = 4.90 N) → ξ = 0.610, H_u = 949 gf ≈ 955 gf = **9.37 N**; measured **9.02 N** (920 gf); H_s = 1128 gf = **11.06 N**. ## 6. Corbelling: stability of overhanging courses, corbelled arches and domes

### Takeaway
A two-dimensional corbelled stack of equal rigid courses is exactly the harmonic-stack problem (§3): course k from the top may project at most L/(2k) beyond the course below, and with a constant projection d the stack of n courses stands iff d ≤ L/n. "Corbelling theory" for arches adds a second condition — no sliding on any bed joint. Published tests exist (IIT Madras dry-stack corbelled vault based on Ta Prohm; Rovero & Tonietti field cases of corbelled domes) but their numbers were not retrievable; Rovero & Tonietti's conclusion is that plain 2-D corbelling theory *fails* for domes because hoop (ring) action carries load, which is important for an engine: a 3-D corbelled dome is *not* a benchmark for the 2-D harmonic rule.

### Cited Findings
- Corbel arch mechanics summary: a corbelled arch "is constructed by offsetting successive courses of stone from each supporting side so that they project towards the symmetry axis"; it requires "two key safety conditions: safety against overturning due to overhanging block weights, and safety against sliding failure along joints where lateral thrust cannot exceed the shear resistance". — [Taylor & Francis knowledge page, Corbel arch](https://taylorandfrancis.com/knowledge/Engineering_and_technology/Civil%2c_environmental_and_geotechnical_engineering/Corbel_arch)
- Experiment: "Structural behaviour of dry-stack stone corbelled vaults under lateral support movement" (IIT Madras; Springer Netherlands). "The test model chosen was based on one of the existing entrance vaults of Ta Prohm Temple, Cambodia, restricted to a bare arch form as a 2D representation"; photogrammetry tracked block displacements; conclusion: "corbelled arch systems under relative in-plane outward support movement tend to fail by overturning inwards, with rocking failure being dominant over sliding due to the structural configuration and available sliding friction." — [IITM publications record](https://publications.iitm.ac.in/publication/structural-behaviour-of-dry-stack-stone-corbelled-vaults-under) (site unreachable at time of research; details from the search index)
- Rovero, L. & Tonietti, U. (2014). "A modified corbelling theory for domes with horizontal layers." *Construction and Building Materials* 50, 50–61, DOI 10.1016/j.conbuildmat.2013.08.032: "corbelling theory does not interpret structural behavior of false domes"; the modified theory models "actions along dome parallels through the collaboration among infinitesimal meridian wedges"; introduces a "stability angle" with closed-form expressions "for cone-shaped domes"; validated against two real case studies. — [FLORE record](https://flore.unifi.it/handle/2158/820494)
- Rovero, L. & Tonietti, U. (2012). "Structural behaviour of earthen corbelled domes in the Aleppo's region." *Materials and Structures* 45, 171–184, DOI 10.1617/s11527-011-9758-1: "the key factor is the horizontal action along the parallels of the dome, produced by engagement and friction between blocks or from mortar cohesion". — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1617%2Fs11527-011-9758-1)
- The classical overhang bound and the balance definition (vertical push-only contact forces, equilibrium of every block) that make the 2-D corbel problem exactly the harmonic stack: see §3 — [Paterson & Zwick](https://arxiv.org/abs/0710.2357)

### Inferences
- **Benchmark I — corbelled "half-arch" (exact, 2-D).** n equal courses of length L overhanging inward with projections p_k: stands iff for every k the centre of mass of courses 1..k (from the top) lies over course k+1, i.e. Σ_{i≤k} p_i ≤ (L/2)·H_k … equivalently the harmonic bound per course p_k ≤ L/(2k) at equality for all k gives the maximum total projection (L/2)H_n. Constant projection d: stands iff d ≤ L/n (derivation in §3). Sliding: with level beds and vertical loads only, there is no shear on any bed joint, so friction does not enter — assert that the result is μ-independent. Instance: L = 0.60 m, n = 6 stones, d = 0.10 m = L/6 → exactly critical; d = 0.11 m → the top five stones rotate about the inner top edge of stone 6 (bottom), stone 6 stays.
- **Two half-arches meeting at the apex with a capstone** is still exact if the capstone is symmetric and rests on both leaves: each leaf then carries half the capstone weight at its tip — treat it as a point load at the tip of course 1 and recompute CoM. Asymmetric support (e.g. one leaf a little lower so the capstone bears on one side) is a sensitive case and not a clean benchmark.
- **Corbelled domes are not 2-D benchmarks:** Rovero & Tonietti show ring compression between adjacent blocks of a course stabilises overhangs beyond the meridian-wedge (2-D) limit; an engine with friction/cohesion between blocks in the same course will (correctly) stand domes the 2-D rule says fall.

### Gaps
- No closed-form result specific to corbelled *arches* by Rovero & Tonietti was found; their work is on domes (meridian-wedge "modified corbelling theory" with a stability angle). The 2019 *Curved and Layered Structures* paper (DOI 10.1515/cls-2019-0003) that appeared in results could not be opened (HTTP 405) and its authorship/content is unconfirmed.
- The IIT Madras corbelled-vault test geometry, block sizes, friction coefficient and measured collapse displacement were not retrievable (site down).
- No published measurements of a plain 2-D corbelled stack at its harmonic limit were found.

## 7. Coulomb friction wedges and blocks held by friction against two faces

### Takeaway
Textbook wedge statics gives exact closed forms: a wedge of half-angle α between two surfaces with friction angle φ = arctan μ is self-locking when α ≤ 2φ (sum of the two friction angles), the force to drive it follows from two free-body diagrams with each contact resultant inclined at φ to its normal, and a worked instance from an open textbook is available (10 lb block, 25° wedge, μ = 0.20 → P = 9.35 lb to raise, 0.436 lb to hold). No primary experiment reporting measured wedge forces was found.

### Cited Findings
- Baker & Haynes, *Engineering Statics: Open and Interactive*, §9.3 Wedges: "a wedge is a tapered object which converts a small input force into a large output force"; two regimes — low-friction splitting wedges and "High-friction (self-locking) wedges [that] control the location of objects or hold them in place". Worked example 9.3.2: minimum force P to raise a 10 lb block with a 25° wedge, μ_s = 0.20 on all surfaces → **P = 9.35 lb**; example 9.3.5: minimum force to prevent the wedge slipping out → **P′ = 0.436 lb**. — [engineeringstatics.org Ch. 9 wedges](https://engineeringstatics.org/Chapter_09-block-and-wedge-friction.html); mirrored at [LibreTexts 9.1.03 Wedges](https://eng.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Mechanical_Engineering/Engineering_Statics%3A_Open_and_Interactive_(Baker_and_Haynes)/09%3A_Friction/9.01%3A_Friction/9.1.03%3A_Wedges)
- Lecture notes following Meriam & Kraige: "A wedge will remain in place with a finite range of possible angular positions if it is self-locking, and simultaneous slippage is not possible if α ≤ 2φ, where α is the wedge angle and φ is the friction angle"; "The friction angle for limiting static friction is given by φ = tan⁻¹ μ"; "When sliding of a wedge is impending, the resultant force on each sliding surface of the wedge will be inclined from the normal to the surface by an amount equal to the friction angle." Worked example posed: "A 5° wedge adjusts the position of a 500-kg rectangular concrete block, with a coefficient of static friction of 0.30 for both wedge surfaces and 0.60 between the block and horizontal surface" — find minimum P. — [Al-Mustaqbal Univ. statics lecture (Meriam-based)](https://www.uomus.edu.iq/img/lectures21/MUCLecture_2025_21913344.pdf)
- Related chapter on blocks pushed at a height (slip vs tip rule, P_slip = μ_s N, tip when N reaches the corner): see §1. — [Baker & Haynes](https://engineeringstatics.org/Chapter_09-slipping-vs--tipping.html)

### Inferences
- **SI conversion of the Baker–Haynes instance:** W = 10 lbf = **44.5 N**; P_raise = 9.35 lbf = **41.6 N**; P_hold = 0.436 lbf = **1.94 N**; wedge angle 25°, μ = 0.20 (φ = 11.31°). Since 25° > 2φ = 22.6°, this wedge is *not* self-locking — consistent with a positive holding force being needed. With μ = 0.25 (φ = 14.04°, 2φ = 28.1° > 25°) the same wedge becomes self-locking and P_hold ≤ 0: a clean two-case benchmark (one wedge pops out when released, the other stays).
- **Closed form for a symmetric wedge of half-angle α driven into a V-slot** (two identical faces, each with friction angle φ): driving force P_in = 2N(sin α + μ cos α) = W_eq·tan(α + φ) per unit normal reaction pair; the wedge stays put without external force iff α ≤ φ per face — i.e. the total included angle 2α ≤ 2φ, the "α ≤ 2φ" statement above where α denotes the included angle. Standard derivation (each contact resultant at φ to the normal; the two resultants must balance with no external load — possible only if the included angle is within the friction cones).
- **Block held between two vertical faces by friction alone (clamping):** a block of weight W squeezed by equal normal forces N from two faces with μ each stays iff 2μN ≥ W (exact, CF). Instance: W = 100 N, μ = 0.4 → N ≥ 125 N per face. If the clamping force comes from the wedge geometry itself (e.g. a keystone-like trapezoid of included angle 2α seated between two rigid faces under its own weight), the block drops unless α ≤ φ — the same self-locking criterion; a trapezoid with 2α = 20° and μ = 0.15 (φ = 8.5°) falls, with μ = 0.20 (φ = 11.3°) stays.
- **Which body moves:** in the raising example the block translates vertically and the wedge translates horizontally; in the "insufficient friction" clamp case the clamped block translates downward with both contacts sliding simultaneously.

## 8. What each benchmark should assert, and how sensitive the answer is to inputs

### Takeaway
Assert *mechanism and threshold*, never displacement: the onset angle/load (to a stated tolerance), the failure mode (rotation about a named edge vs translation along a named plane), and which body or sub-assembly moves while the rest stays. Every scenario above is a one-parameter threshold problem whose answer is linear or arctangent in the inputs, so tolerances of 1–3 % on loads and 0.3–0.5° on angles separate correct from incorrect behaviour; knife-edge cases (harmonic stack, μ = w/h) need a two-sided bracket (stands at (1 − δ), falls at (1 + δ)).

### Cited Findings
- Ochsendorf's model buttresses: theory within "approximately 6%" of measured collapse load, versus a 19–25 % error for the monolithic assumption; failure "characterised … by opening of the joints between masonry blocks" with the buttress "essentially vertical as the applied load is increased" until separation. — [thesis §3.8](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/bitstream/1810/244820/1/Ochsendorf25710.pdf)
- Rocking block: experimental α within 3 % of b/h for h/b = 4 and 6, but 29 % high for h/b = 8 and 14 % low for the chamfered block; the paper attributes the deviations to the real rotation points not being at the corners and notes that "small variations induce large differences in the response of the numerical models due to the high nonlinearity of the problem". — [Prieto et al. 2006](https://repositorium.uminho.pt/server/api/core/bitstreams/82d0aa07-bc14-4ce2-8463-83630b179900/content)
- Tilt-table friction tests: coefficient of variation of the basic friction angle 5–10 %. — [Alejano et al. 2012](https://repositorio.lnec.pt/handle/123456789/1003995)
- Housner small-angle vs exact: the uplift criterion is stated as a/g > α (small angle) by Housner and as ü_g/g ≥ tan α exactly by later authors. — [Housner 1963](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/47803); [Voyagaki et al. 2012](https://www.iitk.ac.in/nicee/wcee/article/WCEE2012_0353.pdf)
- Balance of multiwide stacks is a linear-feasibility question, not a centre-of-mass question; centre-of-mass reasoning gives wrong answers for inverted triangles. — [Paterson & Zwick](https://arxiv.org/abs/0710.2357)

### Inferences
- **Assertion table (what to check, tolerance, assumptions).**
  - §1 incline: onset angle θ₀ = min(arctan μ, arctan w/h) ± 0.5°; mode by comparing μ with w/h; single block. RB, CF.
  - §2 rocking block: no motion at a/g = 0.98·b/h, rotation about the toe at 1.02·b/h; μ must exceed μ_cr (set μ ≥ 1 to isolate). RB, NT, NS.
  - §3 stacks: harmonic — stands at offsets ×0.99, falls at ×1.01; single-block perturbation identifies the moving sub-stack (blocks 1..k). Inverted 2-triangle stands / 3-triangle falls; 4-diamond stands / 5-diamond falls. Offset stack: d = L/n ± 1 %. RB, NT, friction irrelevant.
  - §4 wall: λ₀ = min(t/H, μ) ± 2 %; for a coursed wall the hinge must be at the base joint; sliding at the base only if base μ is the minimum. RB, NT, CF.
  - §5 buttress: capacity between H_u (no-tension continuum) and H_s (monolith), approaching H_u as course height → 0; mode: base-hinge overturning with a detached lower wedge; sliding capacity 0.7(W_above + V) must exceed the overturning capacity. For the scale model: measured 6.96 N and 9.02 N bracketed by H_u and H_s. MT/NT/NS; infinite compressive strength.
  - §6 corbel: d_max = L/n ± 1 %; μ-independence of the result; top n−1 courses move, bottom course stays.
  - §7 wedge: self-locking flips between μ = 0.20 and 0.25 for a 25° wedge; clamped block drops iff 2μN < W.
- **Sensitivity summary.** Linear thresholds (b/h, t/H, μ, d ≤ L/n, 2μN ≥ W): relative error in output = relative error in input. Arctan thresholds: dθ = dμ/(1+μ²) rad. Buttress H_u: ±2 % per ±0.05 in ξ, ±40 kN per ±0.1 in ψ (Benchmark G), −1 % per −0.1° of lean (Goa scale). Contact compliance / finite compressive strength always *lowers* rocking and overturning thresholds (pivot moves inward) — an engine with soft contacts will systematically under-predict §2, §4, §5 thresholds; the Peña h/b = 8 block (measured α above theory) is the exception and is attributed to base/foundation non-rigidity, so do not use it to calibrate.
- **Unit traps for this project.** All strengths here are irrelevant (infinite) except friction (dimensionless) and cohesion (must be zero); the only place a stress enters is the buttress kern criterion, which is a *shape* criterion (resultant at b/3), not a strength — so none of these benchmarks exercises the N↔force-unit conversion; they isolate geometry, mass and friction.
