Project overview, 11 October 2026

LoadPath

A small C++ engine that looks at a pile of rigid blocks and answers three questions: does it stand, which joints give way if it does not, and which pieces fall, as one lump or several.

standalone C++20 library · tests run in seconds · built for Unreal/Chaos later · successor to the solver inside DestructionGame

What it is, in one breath

Imagine a brick wall. Each brick is a rigid block with a mass. Between touching bricks there is a joint: a rectangle of contact with a strength profile (how hard you can squash it, pull it apart, slide it, and how much friction it has). LoadPath never looks at shapes or meshes. It only sees masses, joints and strengths, and from those it works out whether gravity and any pushes can be carried by the joints without any of them exceeding its strength.

If every joint can carry its share, the structure stands. If no arrangement of forces works, something has to move, and the engine says which blocks move together as a rigid cluster. The host game (Unreal with Chaos physics) then takes those clusters and lets them fall, smash and bounce. LoadPath never simulates motion itself. It decides; Chaos animates.

Why a separate library? The old solver lived inside the Unreal project, where a test run meant a minute of editor startup and the physics got tangled with game code. LoadPath is a plain library with a test suite that runs in about ten seconds, so a change can be checked hundreds of times a day. The Unreal adapter comes last, as its own module.

How the pieces fit

Four layers, each built and tested on its own. The host feeds in the model, the two "authorities" judge it in different ways, a combiner reconciles them, and the cascade turns the judgement into cracks, breaks and released clusters.

Two ways to judge, and why both

There are two classic ways to ask "does this stand?", and they fail in opposite directions. LoadPath builds both and lets one referee the other.

1. The elastic field (the standing authority)

Treat every joint as four tiny springs, one at each corner of the contact rectangle. Let the blocks settle under gravity. If the springs can carry the load with every joint inside its strength, that is a proof the structure stands. If a corner would be in tension on a dry joint, the spring opens; if it would slide past friction, it slips. The solver keeps releasing corners until it finds a settled state or runs out of ideas.

This is the fast, physical-feeling method, and it gives a rich readout: how hard every joint is working, on which axis (crushing, pulling apart, sliding). Its weakness is that when it cannot find a settled state it does not know whether the structure really falls or whether it just failed to find the answer. So, by a rule set during review, it says Undecided rather than guess, and it reports Mechanism as an opinion rather than a proof.

2. Limit analysis (the collapse certificate)

Forget stiffness. Ask instead: is there any set of joint forces that balances the loads and stays inside every strength limit? That is a linear program. Scale the loads up by a factor t and find the largest t that still works. If the largest t is at least 1, the structure stands. If it is below 1, nothing can hold it and the dual solution of the program tells you the collapse motion: which bodies move and which joints hinge or slide.

Friction makes the strength limit a cone, which a linear program cannot represent exactly, so it is approximated by a polygon twice: once inscribed inside the cone (so a "stands" answer is safe) and once drawn around it (so a "collapses" answer is safe). Between the two is a thin band where the engine says Undecided; raising the polygon count narrows it.

3. The combined authority

One call per structure. It splits the structure into connected components (groups of blocks that touch each other), judges each with the elastic field, and whenever that field says Mechanism or Undecided it asks the certificate about that component alone. The certificate's answer wins, because a provable force field is the standing criterion. When the elastic field said "falls" and the certificate says "stands", that is counted as a disagreement, which is useful data about where the fast method is weak.

A deliberate limit. The certificate uses "associative" friction, a standard simplification in which a sliding joint also lifts slightly. Real joints do not. The consequence is that for some geometries the certificate says Stands a little past the point where the real thing slides. The tetrahedral rock wedge benchmark shows it: the engine switches at 31.1°, physical tests at 33.4°. That 2.3° gap is recorded as a known limit, with a deliberately failing test waiting for the non-associative referee planned in slice 7.

The cascade

A verdict is not yet a collapse. Real structures fail in sequence: one joint cracks, load shifts to its neighbours, another cracks, until a piece has nothing left holding it and falls. The cascade is that loop. It runs in passes, and every crack and break is stamped with its pass number so the host can play the failure back in order.

Two lessons from the old project are built in as rules. First, crack a few joints at a time: cracking every overloaded joint at once once sent damage through an entire warehouse to the far wall, because hundreds of simultaneous cracks is not a state any real building passes through. The cascade cracks only the joints whose overload ratio is in the top band (by default, the top half of the way down from the worst). Second, a released cluster must be a certified mechanism: the bodies in a cluster are the ones the certificate proved move together, grouped across joints that do not hinge, never a "whatever is connected" guess.

What has been built, slice by slice

Work is cut into slices that each end with a reviewed, committed, fully tested piece. The order follows the design brief, with the collapse certificate pulled forward because the cascade needed it before the catalogue did.

1MODEL

The structure and its vocabulary done

Bodies, joints, strength profiles, and a Structure graph that holds them with stable handles.

Major
Validation that refuses bad input and says why (a non-unit normal, a zero-area joint, a joint to a removed body). Handles stay valid when a body is removed: nothing renumbers, so failure stamps can never be invalidated.
Minor
Dead reads fail closed (a removed body reads NaN mass, never zero). Normalisation that survives tiny and huge numbers. Seven strength numbers per profile, so adding a material is adding a row of data.
Units
SI only, inside the library. The old project lost days to a 100× unit factor; here it cannot exist because there is no conversion.
1bFILE + CLI

A structure file and a command line done

A hand-written JSON reader and writer, round-trip tested, and a tiny loadpath_cli that loads a file and prints it. The file is how a scene is saved and how a bug is reported. Slice 3 is extending it to carry crack and break stamps.

2aSTANDING

The elastic standing authority done

The corner-spring field described above, with a precise contract about what it proves.

Major
Four corner springs per joint, six degrees of freedom per body, solved per connected component so a change on one side of a site never alters a reading on the other. Corners open when a dry joint goes into tension and slip when shear passes friction. Readings per joint: a utilisation ratio and the axis that governs it.
Minor
A pivoted factorisation that can detect a rank defect (the off-the-shelf one could not); rotations scaled by a characteristic length so answers do not change with the size of the structure; a restore rule for corners that re-enter contact.
Ruling
It certifies Stands and Overstressed. Mechanism is an opinion. When it cannot settle, it says Undecided and hands over to the certificate. This replaced three rounds of heuristics that each fixed one case and broke another.
4aCERTIFICATE

The collapse certificate done

Limit analysis as a linear program, with its own interior-point solver written on top of Eigen so the library has no external solver dependency.

Major
Friction cones as 16-sided polygons, inscribed and circumscribed; bonded joints with tension and cohesion; crushing bounds. The two solves give the bracket in Figure 2. A collapse comes with the mechanism: which bodies move, how, and which joints hinge.
Minor
A separate small program chooses the single clearest mechanism when the structure fails at zero load (otherwise the solver returns a blend of every possible failure). A listing threshold so numerical residue never names a bystander as "moving". Agreement with an independent oracle on 300 random structures.
Reviews
Four. They caught a rank problem in the first factorisation, a friction over-read on bonded joints, and a mechanism selection that released too much.
4bCOMBINED

The combined authority, the shear cap, and the hard benchmarks done

Closed today after three reviews.

Major
The combined authority: per-component verdicts, each component judged by its own elastic solve (an earlier version inferred them from one global answer and the review showed two ways that went wrong). The shear cap, a seventh strength number, now enters the linear program as a polygon around the shear force, with the rule that a cap of zero means "no shear", never "no limit".
Anchors
The Couplet–Heyman semicircular arch now pins the textbook least thickness exactly (0.107478 of the radius, hinges at the crown, 54.48° and the springings) and its friction switch (slides at 21°, stands at 22°). The Orduña hollow block pile reads a collapse factor of 0.5005 against a published range of 0.427 to 0.553. The 3DEC wedge is pinned at the certificate's own 31.07°, with the gap to the physical 33.36° recorded.
Minor
A seeded property that judges each component alone and whole and demands identical answers and identical readings; a Slow-in-Debug tier so the arch and pile anchors run only in the optimised build; the three tests that had been parked as "deferred to slice 4" came back green through the combined authority.
3CASCADE

Crack, sever, release done

Closed the same day after two reviews and a follow-up. The engine of the design brief's first three slices now exists end to end.

Major
Joint states that move one way only (intact, cracked, severed, removed) with pass stamps and a pass counter that belongs to the structure and travels with the file; a cracked joint reads as a dry joint to both authorities through one effective-profile accessor; per-axis utilisation in every reading; and the cascade loop of Figure 3 with its band rule, cluster grouping and pass budget.
Pinned by tests
A brick sliding off a slope releases as one cluster with its bed severed on pass 1; a two-brick stack sliding at the ground leaves as a single cluster; a bonded pier cracks its base on pass 1 and tips on pass 2; a top band of 0.5 cracks the joints at ratios 3 and 2 but not 1.2; the arch below least thickness releases six voussoir runs between its seven hinges; a second run continues numbering from where the first stopped, even after a save and reload.
Reviews
The first review found that a release writing no stamp did not advance the pass number, that saving reset it, and that a NaN on one axis could hide a crack. All three became tests before they became fixes.
5DATA

The catalogue, the geometry layer, and the structural fuzz done

The first slice above the engine: materials as data, walls as a function, and a random-structure check that the cascade keeps its promises.

Major
A JSON catalogue of joints and fasteners (EN 1996-1-1 brick-and-mortar bed and perpend, two measured mortar beds, C24 timber bearing, an EC5 screw, nail and bolt), every row cited and labelled characteristic or mean, converted to SI exactly once by a loader that refuses a bad row at its line and leaves nothing half-loaded. A fastener row becomes an area profile with no friction, so two screws carry twice the published value whatever contact area the host gives the joint. A box-contact function and a running-bond wall generator (a 10 × 8 wall is 85 bricks, 152 beds and 75 perpends). A seeded fuzz that runs small stacks and walls through the cascade and checks five invariants: every released cluster is certified, stamps match, passes terminate, the remainder re-judges the same, and the file round-trips the end state.
Pinned by tests
A dry wall overturns at thickness over height within 1 % (10 × 8 and 4 × 5); the bonded 4 × 5 wall overturns at its monolith factor (t/H)(1 + ftA/W) = 2.971 to 1 %; a brick on two screws stands at rest and is overstressed at 1 % past twice their shear, through the full authority; the loader refuses unknown keys, non-finite values and zero stiffness; a NaN box gives no contact.
Reviews
The review traced five data errors to the repo's own research notes: the bolt row had halved a per-plane value, the "mean" fastener rows were characteristic ratios, the screw's withdrawal belonged to a 2.5 mm screw, the code's flexural tension was stored as direct tension (it now enters as fx/3, which makes the bonded wall match the code's moment exactly), and the perpend row described a wall, not a joint. It also found that fasteners carried no stiffness and so could not be used by the elastic field at all. Every one became a test or a recorded ruling.
Found on the way
The elastic field cannot settle on any dry wall with perpends between full bricks: a perpend carrying nothing flips between stuck and slipping forever. The host is safe (the certificate answers), but the next solver slice fixes it.
2cSOLVER

Dry corners at zero load done

A small solver slice forced by slice 5's finding: a dry joint carrying nothing kept the elastic field from ever settling.

Major
A force-noise floor, one millionth of a millionth of the component's own force scale. A corner on a joint with no tension opens only when pulled harder than that; a dry corner inside the band is simply open and reads zero; it closes again only when pushed past twice the floor, so it cannot flutter. A closed tensionless corner inside the band reports zero rather than noise divided by a zero capacity. A structure whose loads overflow the arithmetic reads Undecided, never Stands.
Pinned by tests
Every dry wall at rest, from three bricks to the 85-brick wall, settles in two iterations; the lime-mortar walls (cohesion, no tension) stand at rest; an end push of one ten-millionth of a brick's weight still passes through the perpends and scales linearly; a push of 2 × 10154 newtons, in any direction, never reads Stands.
Reviews
Eight lines of production code took a cold review and two follow-ups. The reviewer built its own probe harness and found an overflow that opened every joint and read a false Stands, a chatter band where a real load sat just under the floor, and guards that would not have caught the mutations they were named for. The fix round found a third defect itself. Each became a test before it became a fix.
Still open
Laterally loaded dry walls can still read Undecided, and the floor has nothing to do with it: a separate stick/slip non-convergence, measured on a grid of 147 points, none of them a false Stands. That is the next solver item after the scale measurement.
6aSCALE

Measure before designing done

Before any scale design, a generator that builds warehouse-sized structures from the slice-5 pieces, and timing rows that say what the engine costs today.

Major
A warehouse generator: four running-bond walls on one ground, a post-and-beam frame on the timber bearing row, planks on the screw row, door and window openings; a shed of 420 pieces, a half warehouse of 1,822, a warehouse of 3,695. A measurement table (claude_plans/SCALE.md) with the elastic solve, the certificate, the full judge and a cascade pass timed at each size.
What the numbers say
The shed judges in half a second; the half warehouse in a minute and a half; a 1,296-brick wall never finishes its first solve. The cost is the dense factorisation, and the hand-written one is 10 to 17 times slower than a library one at the same size. The judge solves the whole structure and then every component again. The certificate is never called when the elastic field decides. Those four facts are the whole of the 6b plan.
Found on the way
A wall with no support left was being cut into single bricks by the cascade, because at a collapse load of zero every motion is a mechanism and the certificate picked a messy one. Now a component with no path to the ground and no host load falls rigid as one piece. The review then showed the rule is exact only under gravity, and that two measurement rows had compared a bonded wall with a dry one.
Reviews
One cold review (nine blocking findings, among them the physics limit of the rigid fall, a rounding-residue collapse, a benchmark of the factorisation, and measurement rows that could never finish and blocked the slow tier) and two follow-ups. Each finding became a test before a fix.

How we know it is right

The project is test-driven without exception: no production code is written until a test for it has been run and seen to fail for the right reason. Three things make the tests worth trusting.

  • Known answers from the literature, never from the old project. Every pinned number has a source row recording where it came from and how it was derived: Housner's rocking block, the sliding and tipping incline, the Paterson–Zwick overhang stacks, the harmonic stack, the Itasca slope regimes, the Couplet–Heyman arch, the Orduña pile, the 3DEC wedge. The arch answer, for instance, is Milankovitch's 1907 result, confirmed by three independent papers.
  • Independent oracles. A small, slow, obviously-correct solver in the test tier checks static admissibility on hundreds of seeded random structures. Seeded properties check that answers do not depend on what sits ten metres away, and that judging a component alone or as part of the whole gives identical readings.
  • Mutation records. A test that passes the moment it is written proves nothing by itself, so for each such test a deliberate bug is introduced into the production code, the test is shown to catch it, and the bug is reverted. The commit messages carry those records.

Known limits, written down

  • Associative friction. The certificate can read Stands slightly past where a real joint slides (the wedge's 2.3° band). The plan is a non-associative referee in slice 7.
  • Undecided is a real answer. Rather than guess, the engine says it cannot tell, and the host keeps the structure standing. The band is narrowed by polygon count and by the certificate refereeing the elastic field, but it exists.
  • Cost. The combined authority copies the structure once per component and solves twice. Fine for test fixtures, not for a 5,000-piece warehouse. Slice 6 is scale.
  • The pile's bond is reconstructed. The paper's figure could not be read, so the block layout follows the published dimensions and block count; the assertion is the published bracket only.
  • Laterally loaded dry walls can still read Undecided. At rest every dry wall now settles, but under a sideways load the free active set can fail to converge on walls of five bricks and more. The combined authority still answers through the certificate; the fix is the next solver item.
  • Scale is not there yet. The engine is right on fixtures and slow on buildings: a 1,822-piece half warehouse takes a minute and a half to judge once, against a design budget of a fifth of a second. The measurement says exactly where the time goes, and 6b spends it in that order.
  • Data is only as good as its citation. Several catalogue numbers survived two agents and were caught by the reviewer against the repo's own notes. Every row now says what it is, where it came from, and how it was converted.

How the work gets done

Three specialised agents, always in the same order: a test writer that produces the failing test, a developer that refuses to write code until it has seen the test fail, and a reviewer with no write access that is expected to reject work. The orchestrator writes contracts, takes rulings, and records them in the design document so no decision lives only in a conversation.

Today's speed trial moved the test and development agents to low reasoning effort while keeping the reviewer at high. Slice 4b, larger than 4a, went from contract to passed review in about four hours. The low-effort test writer still caught three errors in the brief it was handed, and the reviewer's high effort is what found the defects that mattered.
