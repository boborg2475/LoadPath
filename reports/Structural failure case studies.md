# Real Collapses and Solver Traps Become Tests

Of the roughly thirty real collapses surveyed, **seven carry every input a validation test needs** — the Liberton High School wall (3.65 × 2.3 × 0.14 m, 1865 kg, cracked at its base, tipped by two children), the Wallsend chimney felling (81 m, cut through 61 % of its ring, fell on the marked line), the Emilia 2012 precast seats (μ = 0.09–0.13 against spectral accelerations of up to 1 g), the Christchurch parapet census (959 parapets, 230 mm thick, binned by PGA), the Ronan Point joint pressures (failed at 12–21 kPa under a blast of under 69 kPa), the Psycharis Parthenon-type column (12 drums, 1.65 → 1.28 m over 10.08 m) and the Oxgangs gable leaf (102.5 mm brick, 30.8 m/s gust, wall ties without embedment). Another eight — the Minnesota parapet, the Virginia chimney, the Philadelphia Market Street wall and the four UK alteration collapses — support assertions about **which element falls first and in which direction** but give no numbers, and four famous towers (Pavia, Noto, St Mark's, Pisa) are **negative controls that a rigid-block engine must report as standing**, because they failed by crushing, creep or soil, not by statics. On the solver side the documented failure modes split into conventions the engine must declare before any number can be asserted — the Coulomb flow rule (associative answers **0.6 % to 91 %** high), the arch hinge tangency (Heyman **1.4 % unsafe**, discrete voussoirs **up to 17 % conservative**), the arch radius normalisation (**5.7 %**) — and outright bugs with exact unit tests: a joint normal taken from the centroid line, a half-overlap bed joint counted at full area, fewer than **five contact points per edge** (torsion error under 3 % at five, overestimated at three), and non-determinism under block renumbering or thread count, which MuJoCo and Jolt test by **byte-exact comparison**. Almost every field case is strength-free (friction and geometry only), so the one field case that exercises the engine's stress-to-force path is the Liberton counterfactual with a bonded base joint, where 0.1 MPa of tension roughly doubles the push required. The adoption order that follows from this is contact geometry first, the exact field anchors second, convention declarations third, invariance fourth, support-removal topology fifth, and the dynamic fragility and strength-path cases last — each phase chosen so that a failure in it cannot be mistaken for a failure in a later one.

## Seven collapses publish every input a test needs

The Liberton privacy wall is the cleanest rigid-block overturning case in any investigation report because the Fatal Accident Inquiry published the geometry, the mass, the pre-existing crack and the experts' tipping displacement. The wall was "some 3.65ms in length and 2.3ms high", "built of unreinforced single brick masonry approximately 4 inches or 105mm thick" with terrazzo render bringing it to about 140 mm, "supported on its bottom edge or foundation but is not connected in any way to any other part of the room", and "the weight of the wall that fell was approximately 1865kg" ([FAI 2017, paras 16, 24](https://www.scotcourts.gov.uk/media/of2fhcls/fai-keane-grace-wallis-bennett.pdf)). The experts found it "had been cracked" along a horizontal plane near the base, that "the wall would remain stable by virtue of its own weight unless lateral pressure was applied", and that it "would have to tilt approximately 130 to 140mm at its top edge before it tilted far enough to fall" ([FAI 2017, paras 28–31](https://www.scotcourts.gov.uk/media/of2fhcls/fai-keane-grace-wallis-bennett.pdf)). That 130–140 mm is exactly the wall's thickness, which is what a rigid block pivoting about its toe gives when its centroid passes over the pivot — the inquiry's experts and the rigid-block model agree without either knowing about the other. The sheriff's finding that two pupils bracing between the walls "would probably be able to exert sufficient force", one pupil "a possibility only", and girls leaning on it "would not create enough force to cause it to move or tilt" ([FAI 2017, paras 31–32](https://www.scotcourts.gov.uk/media/of2fhcls/fai-keane-grace-wallis-bennett.pdf)) brackets the quasi-static threshold from both sides. Deriving from those published inputs (W = 18.3 kN, t = 0.14 m, h = 2.3 m), a top push of **W·t/2h ≈ 0.56 kN** or about **1.07 kN at a 1.2 m push height** tips the cracked wall; that is derived arithmetic, not an inquiry figure, but it sits where the inquiry's "two probably, one possibly" sits. The test therefore asserts four things: the wall stands under self-weight; the base joint is the only hinge the engine opens (the expert's own reasoning, that leverage "increases the further that mortar bed is situated below the point of application", is the mechanism statement); the top-force threshold lands within a few percent of W·t/2h; and a load of a few tens of newtons produces no mechanism at all. This is the free-wall λ = t/H anchor from the benchmark catalogue in field clothing, with λ = 0.061 at 140 mm or 0.046 at the 105 mm brick core, and it adds one thing the catalogue lacks: a counterfactual in which the base joint keeps even the Eurocode characteristic flexural bond of 0.10 MPa, where the moment f_t·Z over a 3.65 m length adds roughly 1.2 kN·m to the 1.3 kN·m of self-weight resistance and the push needed roughly doubles (derived). The inquiry's point that the pre-existing crack was necessary becomes the one field test that exercises the engine's stress-to-force conversion — the path on which, as the benchmark report observed, a 100× unit error passes every geometry benchmark untouched.

The Wallsend chimney is the only documented felling with the cut fully dimensioned. Reid's 1899 account gives a 266 ft stack with a 21 ft outside and 14 ft inside diameter at the ground, 14 ft and 12½ ft at the top, brickwork 3½ ft thick at the bottom and 9 in at the top; the workmen cut out the walls "about 3 feet from the ground", replacing brick with tarred timber blocks, until "About 40 feet was cut out, and the chimney being at this point 66 feet in circumference, about 26 feet was left at the back", and "6 minutes after the lighting of the fire, the chimney fell exactly on the line marked out for on the ground" ([Reid 1899, DMM transcription](http://www.dmm.org.uk/transime/u17c-01.htm)). In SI that is a frustum **81.1 m tall, base OD 6.40 m with t = 1.07 m, top OD 4.27 m with t = 0.23 m, cut at z = 0.91 m through 61 % of the circumference leaving a 39 % arc** uncut. The test builds the frustum from courses with zero joint tension, replaces the cut arc with rigid props, and asserts that the stack stands with props present, and that with props removed the self-weight resultant falls outside the footprint of the residual arc so the stack rotates about the uncut hinge toward the cut's axis of symmetry — the fall direction, within a few degrees, is the assertable outcome. A two-sided companion follows from the geometry alone: for a ring, the convex hull of the residual arc contains the axis only when that arc exceeds half the circumference, so a cut leaving **55 % should stand and 39 % must fall** (derived; the stand side is not an observed event). The fall of a toppling chimney has one more published prediction worth asserting if the engine ever carries a fracture criterion: ruptures "can be caused by either shear forces, typically developing near the base, or by the bending of the structure... In the latter case the breaking is more likely to occur between one third and one half of the height" ([Varieschi & Kamiya 2003](https://arxiv.org/abs/physics/0210033)), never at the once-popular centre of percussion.

Emilia 2012 is the case the investigators themselves reduced to a rigid-block friction check. The dominant damage was "the loss of support of structural horizontal elements, due to the failure of friction beam-to-column and roof-to-beam connections", the Mirandola record gave **PGA 0.264 g** with spectral ordinates up to 1 g against a code value of 0.140 g, the neoprene-to-concrete friction coefficient "varies in the range 0.09 – 0.13 for compressive stress varying between 1.7 MPa and 5.3 MPa", and "the loss of support mechanism is immediately checked comparing the friction coefficient with the acceleration spectral ordinates in g" — a safety factor μ/(Sa/g) that "is much below 1 for a wide range of periods", so low that even μ = 0.2–0.4 "would not have avoided loss of support" ([Magliulo et al. 2014](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1474491/1/2014%20Magliulo%20et%20al_Earth%20Spectra_Emilia%20eqk.pdf)). The seat length is codified: the support "must be not smaller than 8 cm + l/300", and the 1965 circular's T/N ≤ 0.35 implicitly assumed μ = 0.35 ([Ercolino et al. 2014](https://gala.gre.ac.uk/id/eprint/14263/1/14263_ERCOLINO_Emilia_Earthquake_2014.pdf)). The static form of the test is a beam of weight W on a seat with μ = 0.11 under a horizontal body acceleration: sliding begins exactly at a/g = μ, and the engine must say "slides" at 0.264 g with μ = 0.13 and "holds" at the same acceleration with the code's 0.35. The dynamic form, which needs time integration, asserts that cumulative slip over the record exceeds 8 cm + l/300 so the beam loses its seat, and that a dowel with shear capacity or μ ≥ Sa/g keeps it. The case also fixes an input the engine should carry: friction that falls with normal stress, from 0.13 at 1.7 MPa to 0.09 at 5.3 MPa, is a published fact about a real interface, not a modelling nicety.

The Christchurch parapets are the largest population of free-standing rigid blocks with a recorded input. Giaretton and colleagues inventoried 959 URM parapets, "with 60% (580) of them having collapsed during the 2010/2011 Canterbury earthquake sequence"; 48 % were 500–1000 mm high and 38 % under 500 mm, with "a typical thickness equal to 230 mm (two-leaf-thick wall)", and the February 2011 motion reached "0.7 g registered in the Christchurch CBD and 1.0 g registered in Lyttelton" ([Giaretton et al. 2016](https://research.unipd.it/bitstream/11577/3213625/1/Giaretton%20Dizhur%20daPorto%20Ingham%202016%20Post-Earthquake%20Reconnaissance%20of%20Unreinforced%20and%20Retrofitted%20Masonry%20Parapets.pdf)). Their Table 2 is the fragility curve: in September 2010, when most bins were at or below 0.5 g, **104 stood and 15 fell at 0.20–0.25 g and 276 stood against 43 at 0.26–0.50 g**, 13 % collapsed overall; in February 2011, **226 stood and 371 fell in the 0.51–0.75 g bin** and 57 % collapsed overall ([Giaretton et al. 2016, Table 2](https://research.unipd.it/bitstream/11577/3213625/1/Giaretton%20Dizhur%20daPorto%20Ingham%202016%20Post-Earthquake%20Reconnaissance%20of%20Unreinforced%20and%20Retrofitted%20Masonry%20Parapets.pdf)). For a 230 mm parapet between 0.5 and 1.0 m tall the Housner uplift ratio t/h runs from 0.46 down to 0.23, so the quasi-static tilt test asserts overturning at a/g = t/h, which the benchmark catalogue already pins as b/h; what Christchurch adds is the population answer under records. Because a rocking block survives short pulses well above its uplift acceleration, the dynamic assertion is a steep rise in collapse fraction between 0.25 g and 0.5 g — few failures at 0.20–0.25 g, a majority at 0.51–0.75 g — and never a single sharp threshold. The data carry a caveat the test must respect: 27 % of the two-storey failures "collapsed together with out-of-plane failure of the wall below" and only 18 % were a clean "overturning failure of the parapet" ([Giaretton et al. 2016](https://research.unipd.it/bitstream/11577/3213625/1/Giaretton%20Dizhur%20daPorto%20Ingham%202016%20Post-Earthquake%20Reconnaissance%20of%20Unreinforced%20and%20Retrofitted%20Masonry%20Parapets.pdf)), and PGA is a neighbourhood assignment, not a per-wall record, so the bins are brackets. The Royal Commission's hearing that of 41 URM deaths 36 "were as a result of the façade or the walls of these buildings collapsing outwards" ([Royal Commission transcript](https://canterbury.royalcommission.govt.nz/documents-by-key/20111214.1733/$file/TRANS.20111212.pdf)) is the reason this family of tests deserves the trouble.

Ronan Point supplies a pressure chain rather than a geometry. The 22-storey Larsen-Nielsen tower lost its south-east corner after a gas explosion in flat 90 on the 18th floor whose pressure was "less than 10 lb/in²" (under 69 kPa), while "the pressure that could displace the exterior walls" was 3 lb/in² (21 kPa); the sequence was "southeast corner walls on floors 19 to 22 collapsed", then "floor 18 was overloaded and collapsed onto floor 17, progressing to ground level" ([Penn State failure case](https://www.engr.psu.edu/ae/thesis/failures/MKP/failures/failures.wikispaces.com/Ronan_Point.html)). Pugsley's full-scale test of the H2 joint failed at 1.8 psi (12.4 kPa) against a calculated 1.4 psi ([Building.co.uk](https://www.building.co.uk/comment/back-issues/3053297.article)), the connections "relied, in large part, on friction" and the tower "lacked alternate load paths" ([Pearson & Delatte 2005](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%290887-3828%282005%2919%3A2%28172%29)), and the inquiry's lasting output was the alternate-path rule and a local-resistance requirement of 5 psi, which NBS records as 34 kN/m² ([NBSIR 76-1106](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/IR/nbsir76-1106.pdf)). As a test this is a topology case with a bracket: a stack of panel-and-slab storeys with friction-only, tension-free joints must lose the pressurised flank panel somewhere between 12 and 21 kPa — the various psi figures refer to different elements and must not be merged — and once the panel is gone the four storeys above must lose bearing and the collapse must stay confined to the one bay while the rest of the tower stands. The same NBS report holds the "stands" variant: a panel building in Algeria "lost several panels due to a bomb explosion but did not collapse" because the remaining elements had strength and continuity ([NBSIR 76-1106](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/IR/nbsir76-1106.pdf)), so giving the slab–wall joints modest tensile ties must let the engine bridge the lost panel. Panel thicknesses and the H2 joint geometry are not in the open sources, so the absolute pressure needs an assumed bearing load; the confinement and the tie-versus-no-tie flip do not.

The Psycharis column is the published geometry for drum columns. The authors analysed a column with "total height of 10.08 m, being composed of a shaft of 9.38 m and a capital... diameters ranging from 1.65 m at the base to 1.28 m at the top. The shaft was divided into 12 drums of different height according to actual measurements of the columns of the Pronaos", in 3DEC with Coulomb friction, joint stiffness 1 GPa/m, no damping during rocking and no shear connectors between drums ([Psycharis, Fragiadakis & Stefanou 2013](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.4484)). The static tilt threshold for a monolith of that envelope is about **atan(1.65/10.08) ≈ 9.3°**, a static a/g of about 0.16 (derived; the taper lowers the centroid and raises it slightly), and the static test asserts that the engine opens the joint the DEM opens. The mechanism assertions come from the pulse literature: "Drums slide relative to each other even when the ground acceleration's g-value is below the interface friction coefficient", free drums "respond more controllably than monolithic columns of the same size and slenderness", and "stiff metallic shear links in-between column drums may have an undesirable role" ([Konstantinidis & Makris 2005](http://www.witpress.com/elibrary/wit-transactions-on-the-built-environment/81/15050)); shake-table marble models "hardly deformed permanently under the strongest motions recorded in Greece", with collapse "probable only for extremely harsh, directivity-affected motions" and "Unlike PGA, maximum spectral displacement and the length scale L were effective intensity measures" ([Drosos & Anastasopoulos 2014](https://discovery.dundee.ac.uk/en/publications/shaking-table-testing-of-multidrum-columns-and-portals/)). The friction angle Psycharis used did not survive text extraction and no real colonnade has a documented collapse with a recorded input, so this family asserts behaviour — no uplift below 0.16 g, drum sliding before overturning under high-frequency pulses, the jointed column outlasting its monolith under long pulses — not a collapse PGA.

Oxgangs rounds out the seven as a stand/fall pair on one wall. During Storm Gertrude "a substantial section of the upper level of the external brickwork face of a non-structural cavity wall to a two-storey gable of the school, weighing approximately 9 tons, collapsed onto the pathway below"; the build-up was a 140 mm inner leaf, a 120 mm cavity and a 102.5 mm outer leaf with ties specified at 50 mm embedment; the Met Office "recorded gusts of up to 69 mph around the time of the collapse of the wall, which were at a level 20% lower than the design requirements"; and the cause was "poor quality construction... which failed to achieve the required minimum embedment of 50mm for the wall ties", so that "the outer face of brick was simply pulled away from the inner leaf", while a wall built to standard "would have been able to withstand the level of wind loading" ([Cole report 2017](https://www.ciob.org/media/113/download)). Modelled as a 102.5 mm leaf of two-storey height pinned at its base only, under a 30.8 m/s gust (a stagnation pressure of about 0.6 kPa at 1.25 kg/m³, derived), the untied leaf's overturning pressure γ·t²/h is of order 0.03 kPa (derived), twenty times below the demand, and it must fall; with ties at embedment acting as lateral restraints the same leaf must stand. The gap between 0.03 and 0.6 kPa is so wide that this is a wiring test for restraint elements, not a threshold test.

## Mechanism-only cases and negative controls fence the engine's scope

Eight further collapses are rich in mechanism and poor in numbers, and they are worth adopting as direction-and-sequence assertions because that is the thing blind-prediction exercises show discrete models get right. In Minnesota a two-storey building "had a brick parapet wall which extended approximately four feet above the roof. Removal of the roof resulted in a free standing brick wall which was more than one story in height", and as the second-to-last roof section was lifted "the brick parapet wall collapsed inward and fell on the victim" ([FACE 94MN020](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/167200)); the assertable fact is the inward fall toward the side that had just lost its restraint and received the lift's pull. In Virginia a demolition left "a large brick chimney between 26 and 30 feet high and part of a stone wall", and a co-worker "saw the chimney sway and fall as a single mass, landing directly across the operator's cab" ([FACE 98-18](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/167389)); "as a single mass" discriminates between a mortar with modest tension, which overturns the stack as one body, and a cohesionless stack, which crumbles course by course. In Philadelphia a four-storey unreinforced building under demolition fell onto a one-storey neighbour and "the entire wall section above the height of the thrift store collapsed in a single unit" ([Penn State](https://www.engr.psu.edu/ae/thesis/failures/MKP/failures/failures.wikispaces.com/22nd_and_Market_Street_Salvation_Army_Building.html)) after the contractor "removed critical, structural supports for the wall that collapsed" over three days ([OSHA](https://www.dol.gov/newsroom/releases/osha/osha20131114)); the only height is a press figure of about 30 ft, so the test asserts that with floor joists modelled as restraints the wall stands and with them removed the whole unrestrained section goes as one unit under a small push. The UK prosecutions give the same shape without any geometry: at Windsor "the brick gable wall fell into the building after the roof was removed" ([HSE 2025](https://press.hse.gov.uk/2025/09/29/builder-sentenced-after-house-collapse-injures-three-workers/)); at Bushey a house had "its roof, internal walls, and structural support members for both gable ends" taken out and an unsupported gable fell onto the neighbour ([HSM](https://www.hsmsearch.com/?p=17479)); at Brighton a basement dig left "the gable wall partially collapsed, and the ground floor fell into the basement" ([Construction Index](https://www.theconstructionindex.co.uk/news/view/builder-jailed-over-basement-excavation-collapse)). Each becomes a "which element first" assertion on a generic house model: gable after roof, floors after the central wall is undermined, ground floor into the void after the gable base goes. The free-standing-wall guidance sits alongside these as an ordering test rather than a threshold: the BRE-derived council table gives maximum heights of 525/450/400/375 mm for a 100 mm half-brick wall across wind zones 1–4 and 1450/1300/1175/1075 mm for a 215 mm wall ([Milton Keynes Council](https://www.milton-keynes.gov.uk/sites/default/files/2022-03/garden-walls.pdf)), i.e. h/t limits of 5.25 to 3.75 and 6.7 to 5.0. Those ratios do not imply one critical pressure — γ·t²/h at the zone-1 heights is about 0.34 kPa for the half-brick wall and 0.57 kPa for the one-brick wall (derived) — so the table embeds flexural strength and safety factors, and the safe assertion is that the engine's permissible height is monotonic in thickness and falls monotonically with zone pressure, not that its factor of safety equals one at each entry.

The negative controls matter because the catalogue of famous tower collapses invites exactly the wrong reading. Pavia's Civic Tower "suddenly collapsed on 17 March 1989" with 2.8 m walls of two brick faces around a rubble core, a total weight of 120,000 kN with a 30,000 kN granite belfry, computed compression of 1.7–2.0 N/mm² against prism strengths of 1.5–3.5 N/mm², and "The only clear situation was the high value of compressive stress due to the dead load" ([Binda, Anzani & Saisi 2003](https://www.witpress.com/Secure/elibrary/papers/STR03/STR03010FU.pdf)). Noto's piers "were already damaged in the fifties by large vertical cracks due to high compression stresses" and the 1990 earthquake "was only worsening the situation" ([Binda et al. 2003](https://www.witpress.com/Secure/elibrary/papers/STR03/STR03010FU.pdf)). St Mark's Campanile followed a cut "about 40 cm high and 30 cm deep at the base of the tower", then "a large crack ran almost the full height of the northern side", and "The tower collapsed vertically rather than toppling" ([Wikipedia, citing Distefano and Zanetto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Mark%27s_Campanile)). None toppled; a rigid-block engine with infinite compressive strength must report all three standing — Pavia as a 2.8 m-walled prism under its belfry, St Mark's as a 12 m square, 50 m shaft with a 0.3 m deep notch in one face — and a test that expects anything else has misunderstood what the engine is for. Pisa is the converse control: a foundation that "sloped towards the south at 5.5 degrees" with the seventh floor overhanging "by about 4.5 m" ([Ingenia](https://ingenia.org.uk/articles/the-stabilisation-of-the-leaning-tower-of-pisa/)), whose weight line sits roughly 2.4 m from the centre of a 19.6 m foundation (derived) and whose real hazard was "leaning instability" of the soil. The engine must report a large standing margin; the only route to failure is a yielding foundation, outside rigid-block scope. These four cost almost nothing to write and they document the scope boundary in the test names.

Two further families are promising but blocked on paywalled inputs. The dry-stone retaining walls of Colas, Morel and Garnier — two 2.5 m walls "loaded with gravel backfill until failure" ([Coventry record](https://pureportal.coventry.ac.uk/en/publications/full-scale-field-trials-to-assess-dry-stone-retaining-wall-stabil/)) — and the Bath EPSRC series that "successfully recreated bulging" ([Bath record](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/drystone-retaining-walls-from-full-scale-testing-to-construction-)) would supply failure fill heights once the papers are in hand; until then the only assertable statement is Southampton's that toppling "is likely to occur in a brittle manner, with wall crest deflections not exceeding 1% of the backfill height before the factor of safety drops to about 1.05" ([Southampton eprint](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/39421)), and Burgoyne's four-wall stand/fall split, already in the benchmark catalogue, remains the retaining-wall anchor. The timber-on-masonry question has no investigation report at all; the nearest published physics is that roof thrust "generates a destabilizing effect over motion" while "the roof weight can produce a positive stabilizing effect for rotation amplitudes smaller than the critical value" ([Univ. Cagliari](https://iris.unica.it/handle/11584/189761)), which yields a generic gable test — add roof weight through a wall plate and the overturning margin rises, add rafter thrust and it falls — with no case to calibrate it.

## Solver failure modes divide into conventions to declare and bugs to catch

The largest documented error in rigid-block limit analysis is not a bug but a choice, and the test suite has to treat it as one. With Coulomb friction the associative solution is an upper bound; Hua and Milani found that "in some cases an incorrect collapse mechanism and an overestimated ultimate load would be obtained through associated limit analysis", with the associated load **21 % higher** in their reinforced-arch benchmark, up to **70.5 %** over a friction-angle sweep and up to **91 %** over a cohesion sweep ([Hua & Milani 2023](https://re.public.polimi.it/retrieve/85bd1ffc-ed45-4f13-9c18-5dc3a3e551e0/2023_CAS.pdf)). The non-associative answer is not unique either: Orduña and Lourenço state that "A constant in all these investigations is the lack of unique solutions for limit analysis problems in the presence of non-associated flow rules", and minimising the load factor "can severely underestimate the ultimate load factor" — 10.8 %, 25.8 % and 40 % below FEM in their three examples, once with a mechanism "very different from the previous ones" ([Orduña & Lourenço 2005](https://hdl.handle.net/1822/4600)). The benchmark report already pins the two reference problems — the Ferris & Tin-Loi walls (associative 0.64286 → 0.23964, non-associative 0.6 % to 25.9 % lower) and the hollow pile (λ ∈ [0.427, 0.553], load-path 0.500) — so this report adds the diagnostic rather than the fixture: the overestimate is small when the mechanism rocks and large when it slides, so the engine should report the share of dissipation on sliding modes and the test should assert that λ_assoc/λ_nonassoc exceeds one on a sliding-dominated specimen and shrinks toward one as pre-compression rises. The Foraboschi wall gives the specimen: associative and non-associative differences "are relevant for low value of the vertical load only, when sliding mechanism is predominant", and raising μ from 0.6 to 0.75 at 40 kN pre-compression flips the mechanism from sliding to rocking ([Portioli et al. 2012](https://www.iitk.ac.in/nicee/wcee/article/WCEE2012_4233.pdf)). The arch friction sweep is the second convention test: purely rotational collapse of the semicircular arch appears only for μ > 0.396 and purely sliding collapse below μ = 0.31 ([Rizzi, Rusconi & Cocchetti](https://aisberg.unibg.it/retrieve/handle/10446/30274/903737/ERizziESPaperDDA14-Accepted.pdf)), and an associative solver must not report a standing arch at μ = 0.2, because dilatant sliding manufactures capacity. Casapulla's observation that flat arches "would never collapse unless sliding occurred" ([Casapulla 2001](https://www.witpress.com/elibrary/wit-transactions-on-the-built-environment/55/3289)) gives the simplest negative test of all: a five-voussoir flat arch with μ = 0.6 under self-weight and a point load must return a finite load factor by sliding, and a no-sliding solver returns infinity.

The arch hinge problem is where small conventions produce errors in both directions, and the suite has to resolve them separately or they cancel. The published semicircular least-thickness values are Heyman's **t/R = 0.105965 with the intrados hinge at 58.83°**, the CCR value 0.107426 at 54.50°, and Milankovitch's 0.107478 at 54.48°; Heyman's answer "does not collocate itself on the true (rather flat) peak of the curve", the difference "rather sits in the different tangency conditions assumed from scratch at the haunches' intrados" and not in centreline weight, and "the flatness of the peak explains the rather insensitive effect of the inner hinge position β in stating the correct least thickness" ([Rizzi et al.](https://aisberg.unibg.it/retrieve/handle/10446/30274/903737/ERizziESPaperDDA14-Accepted.pdf)). So a 1.4 % thickness error comes with a 4.3° hinge error, and the hinge angle is the sharper diagnostic. Discretisation pulls the other way: DDA arches of 12 to 90 blocks give results that "are always above the theoretical predictions and tend to diverge from them, almost linearly, at increasing number of blocks", with "a value of about 17% for the case of 90 blocks (with 2° opening), while more confined differences below 7% are recorded for... openings that are larger than 5°", and only about 4.5 % with pre-imposed hinge joints ([Rizzi et al.](https://aisberg.unibg.it/retrieve/handle/10446/30274/903737/ERizziESPaperDDA14-Accepted.pdf)). Stereotomy shifts the answer again — vertical cuts give 0.1095 ([Makris & Alexakis 2013](https://research.aston.ac.uk/en/publications/the-effect-of-stereotomy-on-the-shape-of-the-thrust-line-and-the-)) — and normalising by the intrados radius instead of the centreline inflates t/R by 1/(1 − t/2R) ≈ 5.7 % at t/R = 0.107 (derived), larger than the Heyman–Milankovitch gap. The tests follow directly. A continuum-limit arch must give **0.1075 ± 0.001 with the hinge at 54.5° ± 1°**, where 0.106 at 58.8° means the resultant-tangency simplification is in use and 0.1095 means vertical cuts. A voussoir-count sweep over 12, 18, 24, 36, 60 and 90 blocks must stay at or above 0.1075 and within 7 % for voussoirs of 5° or more, and any result below 0.1075 at any count is a false-stand. Finite crushing, added by thickness reduction, gives "the obvious reduction of the load factor" with "a negligible influence on the hinge locations" ([Clemente et al. 2023](https://iris.enea.it/retrieve/68a3789d-468d-482f-8b94-2cffdd24cadd/applsci-13-07509-v2.pdf)), so re-running with a strength must lower λ and move hinges by less than one voussoir. And an arch-bridge fixture with a rigid ring must carry a documented bias flag: against the Prestwood test the rigid ring "overestimates the collapse load by about 46.3%" while a deformable ring lands within 1 % ([Hua & Milani 2024](https://re.public.polimi.it/handle/11311/1279952)).

Contact geometry is where the literature locates the bugs proper, and these are the tests that must exist before any of the above means anything. Pulatsu and co-authors found in 3DEC that with five contact points along each edge "the error is less than 3% in torsion capacity, whereas the coarse contact point discretization overestimates the analytical" solution, recommended "at least five contact points along each edge", and reported that results are "significantly sensitive to defined contact stiffnesses when they are relatively low" ([Pulatsu et al. 2023](https://upcommons.upc.edu/bitstreams/d7493969-187f-4d8a-87ac-787ef67011fb/download)). The common-plane family of detectors has a heuristic normal: the traditional method is "prone to infinite loops in hexahedral contacts" and for edge–edge contacts "contact points deviate from the true positions when the centroid line is not perpendicular to the common plane" ([Liu 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12618607/)). That sentence is the published analogue of the centroid-direction-normal bug, and it bites hardest exactly in running bond, where every bed-joint contact is a half-overlap whose centroid line is tilted; a normal taken from the centroid line or an overlap area counted at full face biases moment capacity upward systematically, not randomly. Itasca's own tolerance note lists what goes wrong when the contact tolerance is too large — "Contacts forming across thin blocks", "Nonplanar block faces", "Normals that cannot be calculated" — and too small, "Thin blocks that collapse during cycling", with the rule that ATOL stay at or under 20 % of the smallest edge and the warning that "The validity of the automatically assigned ATOL decreases as the range in block sizes increases" ([Itasca tolerance note](https://docs.itascacg.com/pfc700/3dec/docproject/source/modeling/problemsolving/tolerance-3dec.html)). Even perfect geometry mismatches experiments: Orduña and Lourenço attribute observed bed-joint sliding absent from their models to "the uneven contact at the interfaces in the experimental models due to geometric tolerances in the size of the blocks" ([Orduña & Lourenço 2005](https://hdl.handle.net/1822/4600)). The unit tests are small and exact: two identical cuboids with the upper shifted by half its length must give a normal of exactly (0, 0, 1), an area of exactly half the face and a moment capacity about the overlap centroid; interval intersections that touch at an edge, nest one footprint inside another, or cross at 90° must return zero, the smaller area and the cross area; three stacked slabs a tenth of the tolerance thick must produce exactly two contacts and none between slabs one and three; a five-course, four-stretcher wall must have every half-overlap bed contact present, a missing one showing up as a wall that rocks as separate columns; and a rectangular dry joint under torsion with 3, 5 and 9 points per edge must converge with the three-point value above the analytical answer, so that a solver which under-predicts at three points is known to have a different bug.

Two physics biases pull in opposite directions and must be isolated on separate fixtures. With infinite compressive strength the Foraboschi wall's predicted mechanism "does not fit the experimental outcome", and at 140 kN pre-compression "extensive crushing is observed at the corners of the right pier" ([Portioli et al. 2012](https://www.iitk.ac.in/nicee/wcee/article/WCEE2012_4233.pdf)); infinite strength is acceptable "for unreinforced masonry with dry or poor-quality mortar" but "crushing could significantly affect collapse of strengthened masonry" ([Portioli, Casapulla & Cascini 2015](https://www.iris.unina.it/handle/11588/645460)). In the other direction, "The use of models not involving the effective interlocking between walls often yields too conservative results in seismic assessment" ([Casapulla et al. 2024](https://iris.unina.it/handle/11588/1038214)). A model can therefore validate on a wall where the two cancel, which is why the suite needs a squat pier under high axial load where f_c = ∞ must give rocking at λ_rock and f_c = 1.21 MPa must change the mechanism to toe crushing and lower λ (a solver returning the same number for both is ignoring crushing — the benchmark catalogue's Vasconcelos piers, rocking at 37–41 kN under 100 kN axial and crushing at 250 kN, are the experimental counterpart), and an L-shaped dry wall bonded through the corner against the same façade with a straight vertical joint, where λ_interlocked must exceed λ_free by a margin that grows with the number of interlocking courses. The Restrepo-Vélez tilt-table programme, 42 configurations varying "wall length, openings, staggering ratio, wall-to-wall connection quality, overburden loads, and number of stories" ([EUCENTRE](https://bookstore.eucentre.it/prodotto/static-tests-on-dry-stone-masonry-and-evaluation-of-static-collapse-multipliers/?lang=en)), is the reference experiment for the interlock test and is already catalogued in the benchmark report with its measured multipliers.

## Invariance tests have byte-exact criteria, physics engines mostly do not

Non-determinism is the solver failure every engine documents and few masonry papers mention, and the published remedies give the test its form. PhysX offers only "limited deterministic simulation" — identical results when "simulating the exact same scene" with the same stepping and release on the same platform, and "the results of the simulation can change if actors are inserted in a different order" ([PhysX docs](https://nvidia-omniverse.github.io/PhysX/physx/5.4.1/docs/RigidBodyDynamics.html)). Jolt is deterministic when "The APIs that modify the simulation are called in exactly the same order" and "The same binary code is used", offers a cross-platform mode verified on 14 builds at about 8 % cost, and ships a "Check Determinism" that records state before each step, rewinds and re-runs ([Jolt docs](https://jrouwe.github.io/JoltPhysics/)). MuJoCo's test is the strictest: `SingleAndMultiThreadedMatch` warms up 22 humanoids, copies the state, runs one copy on a 10-thread pool, and compares every data array with `memcmp`, expecting zero — exact bytes, no tolerance ([MuJoCo engine_thread_test.cc](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google-deepmind/mujoco/main/test/engine/engine_thread_test.cc)). Yade's maintainer states the opposite philosophy for a DEM code: multi-threading "reveals the indeterminism, j=1 hides it, but it is still indeterminate in both cases", and a single thread "should be seen like running a random number generator with always the same seed" ([yade-users](https://archive.lists.launchpad.net/yade-users/msg05917.html)). For a statics engine the analogue of insertion order is block numbering and contact enumeration inside a Gauss–Seidel sweep or an LP basis, and the analogue of thread count is the reduction order of per-block force sums. One subtlety has no masonry citation but is standard LP behaviour: the load factor of an associative LP is unique but the mechanism is not when the optimal face is degenerate, so an interior-point solver returns a blend and a simplex solver a vertex, and two correct solvers can disagree on the mechanism while agreeing on λ to machine precision; Orduña and Lourenço come closest when they note the load-path answer depends on the starting stress state ([Orduña & Lourenço 2005](https://hdl.handle.net/1822/4600)). The tests are four: the same wall with block IDs permuted over at least twenty seeds must give λ identical to 10⁻¹² relative and the same mechanism up to a declared secondary objective; runs on 1, 2, 4 and 8 workers must memcmp equal, or if the design accepts summation noise, agree on λ to 10⁻⁹ and never change the standing/falling verdict; a recorded state must replay step-exactly; and a mid-size fixture must carry a golden λ whose tolerance documents the FMA and contraction policy.

Conditioning is the quieter failure, and the literature gives the signature rather than a threshold. In explicit DEM "the stable timestep is proportional to the square root of the mass of a body divided by the sum of the stiffnesses acting on the body", a few small or stiff bodies "may cause the timestep to be very small", and mass scaling makes "the path... unphysical and the timescale... meaningless" ([Itasca timestep](https://docs.itascacg.com/pfc700/pfc/docproject/source/manual/numerical_simulations_with_pfc/pfc_formulation/timestep_constraints.html)); the DDA arch needed 1000 steps at 24 blocks and 4000 at 180 ([Rizzi et al.](https://aisberg.unibg.it/retrieve/handle/10446/30274/903737/ERizziESPaperDDA14-Accepted.pdf)). In rigid-body spring networks the Bolander–Saito scaling gives elastic uniformity only at zero Poisson's ratio ([FraMCoS-4](https://framcos.org/FraMCoS-4/773.pdf)) and mechanical properties "are strongly dependent on the mesh configuration" ([Eliáš & Vořechovský](https://gruppofrattura.it/ocs/index.php/esis/ECF17/paper/download/6566/3181)). For an implicit engine the failure is loss of digits as k_max/k_min grows; for an explicit one it is blow-up or a wrong dynamic answer that looks like a right static one. The tests: a two-block column swept from k_n = 10⁶ to 10¹⁴ N/m³ must keep its static reaction equal to W to 10⁻⁹ relative and report a condition estimate or refuse beyond a documented ratio; any explicit stepper must assert Δt ≤ 0.5·Δt_crit for the fixture; a Voronoi bar must strain uniformly within 1 % with ν = 0 unless a tangential ratio is set; and a face with 2, 5, 10 and 20 springs must converge in moment capacity within 1 % by 10.

The physics-engine fixtures are worth adopting precisely because most of them come without pass criteria and the few that have them are reusable as written. Itasca's four-regime block — a 10 m wide block on a 40° slope, 2000 kg/m³, with (h, φ) of (10 m, 40.1°) stable, (5 m, 20°) sliding, (25 m, 50°) toppling and (20 m, 30°) both ([3DEC Sliding and Toppling Blocks](https://docs.itascacg.com/itasca930/3dec/docproject/source/examples/SlidingBlock.html)) — is already in the benchmark catalogue; this report adds the assertion that the stable case's centroid moves under 1 mm, since Itasca judges the regime by inspection. NVIDIA's SimReady slope drop states numbers: a 45° slope with μs = 0.5 and μd = 0.4 passes if the box stays within 0.02 m of the ramp and moves at least 0.01 m downhill, and fails on separation above 0.05 m for 0.1 s, on sitting 0.02 m below the ramp plane, or on tunnelling 0.1 m below the floor ([SimReady FET003](https://nvidia.github.io/simready-foundation/2026.08.0/guides/benchmark/tests/fet003/slope-drop.html)); the analytic acceleration g(sin 45° − 0.4 cos 45°) = 4.16 m/s² is the positive check (derived). Chrono's contact-force unit test drops eight 5 kg spheres into a box and requires the container's vertical reaction to satisfy |1 − F_z/392.4 N| < 10⁻³ at every step from 0.5 s to 3 s ([Chrono utest_MCORE_contact_forces](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/projectchrono/chrono/main/src/tests/unit_tests/multicore/utest_MCORE_contact_forces.cpp)) — the simplest load-path assertion there is, and one a statics engine can run without any time integration. The five-sphere pyramid has an exact friction switch: four spheres on the floor and one on top at 45° contacts need μ_sphere ≥ √2 − 1 = 0.41421, and the author proposes testing "with a sphere-to-sphere friction coefficient slightly above 0.41421... and one slightly below... In the first case the pyramid should be stable, and in the second case the top ball should fall to the floor" ([Cogliati, arXiv 1103.6082](https://arxiv.org/abs/1103.6082)); run at 0.43 and 0.40 it catches false sticking and creep in one pair. Box2D's stacking samples — a 12-row vertical stack of 0.9 m rounded boxes at friction 0.3 with a 0.01 m row offset, a 10 × 10 tilted stack, a card house of ~40 cards 2 mm thick at ±25° and friction 0.7, and a 17-voussoir arch at friction 0.6 with four boxes on the keystone ([Box2D sample_stacking.cpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/erincatto/box2d/main/samples/sample_stacking.cpp)) — are visual regressions, and Catto's own solver comparison names the failure they catch: PGS with Baumgarte "can cause energy creation and jitter if not tuned carefully" and some schemes show "tangential oscillations" ([Solver2D](https://box2d.org/posts/2024/02/solver2d/)). They become automatable with a displacement bound (lateral drift under a hundredth of a box over 10 s) and an energy-monotonicity check after settling. Bullet ships no stacking regression in its own test tree, PhysX ships no public unit tests, and the forum record of a stack that "began to tilt and eventually collapse at the sixth piece" in one Bullet version and not the next ([CoppeliaSim forum](https://forum.coppeliarobotics.com/viewtopic.php?p=37210)) is the reason a golden fixture with a stored answer belongs in the suite.

The blind-prediction exercises settle how dynamic cases should be asserted. In the SERA-AIMS stone aggregate, twelve teams using everything from equivalent frames to discrete elements produced results that "varied widely in displacement, interface opening and damage mechanisms, even though material parameters were known. Correct predictions were rare" ([EPFL record](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/309533?ln=fr)); in the SERA groin vault, finite elements "yielded the most accurate predictions regarding displacements and crack patterns" while "a Discrete Element model provided the most accurate prediction of damage mechanisms" ([IJAH 2024](https://cris.pucp.edu.pe/en/publications/shake-table-testing-of-a-brick-masonry-groin-vault-overview-of-bl/)); and the 2025 PEER contest states outright that "it is often impossible to predict the response even of a solitary rocking block. However, it is much easier (and still highly useful) to predict the statistics of the response of a rocking block to a bin of excitations", asking for "The probability of collapse of at least one wall" over 29 motions on virgin specimens ([PEER rules](https://peer.berkeley.edu/sites/default/files/rules_new.pdf)). For this engine that means every dynamic test — Christchurch, Emilia, Psycharis — asserts a mechanism and a collapse fraction over a bin, never a displacement trajectory from a single record, and the Housner restitution model should be replaced by Kalliontzis's, which "is strongly encouraged over Housner's classical one" ([CROSBI](https://www.bib.irb.hr/1108475)).

## Adopt contacts first, field anchors second, dynamics and strength last

The order is set by two rules: a test must not be able to fail for a reason a later test is meant to expose, and a test's inputs must be in hand. Applying them gives six phases, each with a named exit condition.

**Phase 1, contact geometry and load path**, has to come first because every later number rests on it: the half-offset cuboid pair (normal exactly vertical, area exactly half), the three interval-intersection corner cases, the thin-slab tolerance test, the running-bond completeness count, and Chrono's total-reaction-equals-weight check in its static form. These take hours, need no physics beyond equilibrium, and a failure here is unambiguous. The exit condition is that a five-course wall reports every contact with the right normal and area.

**Phase 2, exact field anchors**, adopts the real collapses whose answers coincide with the benchmark catalogue's exact tier so that the field case and the closed form check each other: Liberton (base hinge only, 0.56 kN top push, nothing at tens of newtons), the Christchurch parapet under quasi-static tilt at t/h = 0.23–0.46, the Psycharis column under static tilt at about 9.3°, the Oxgangs leaf tied and untied, and the four negative controls — Pavia, Noto and St Mark's standing with infinite compressive strength, Pisa standing with a 2.4 m eccentricity on a 9.8 m half-width. The BRE height table joins as a monotonicity sweep. Everything here is friction-and-geometry only, so a failure is a statics bug, not a convention. The exit condition is that the engine's hinge choice and threshold on a single free wall match the inquiry to a few percent.

**Phase 3, convention declarations**, is where the engine must say what it is before a number can be asserted. The Couplet–Heyman arch at 0.1075 ± 0.001 with its hinge at 54.5° ± 1°, the voussoir-count sweep, the named radius, the arch friction sweep (rotation above μ = 0.40, sliding below 0.31), the flat arch with finite λ by sliding, and the flow-rule pair already in the catalogue — Ferris & Tin-Loi Examples 1 and 4 with the Orduña pile — each written with the convention in the test's name. The Liberton bonded-base counterfactual belongs here too, because it is the first test that converts a stress to a force and therefore the first that can catch a unit error. The exit condition is that an associative engine matches the LP column and a non-associative one lands inside the published brackets, with the sliding share of dissipation reported.

**Phase 4, invariance and conditioning**, follows the conventions because a permutation or thread test is only meaningful once the answer it protects is right: block-ID permutation to 10⁻¹² over twenty seeds, thread memcmp or a 10⁻⁹ λ tolerance with an unchanged verdict, state replay, a golden mid-size λ, and the stiffness-ratio sweep with a reported condition estimate. The exit condition is a stored golden answer that survives renumbering and a change of thread count.

**Phase 5, support-removal topology**, adopts the cases whose assertion is sequence and direction: the Wallsend frustum (stands on props, falls toward the cut, 55 % residual stands and 39 % falls), the Ronan Point stack (panel lost in the 12–21 kPa bracket, four storeys above lose bearing, collapse confined to the bay, ties bridge it), the Philadelphia wall (stands with joists, falls as one unit without), the Minnesota parapet (inward), the Virginia chimney (as a single mass under modest joint tension, crumbling without), and the generic house with gable-after-roof and floors-after-central-wall ordering. These need a restraint element and a load-redistribution pass but no dynamics. The exit condition is that removing a restraint reproduces the documented first element and direction in every case.

**Phase 6, dynamics and strength**, is last because it requires capabilities the earlier phases do not — a time stepper, a pulse or record driver, a crushing law — and because its answers are bins rather than numbers: the Emilia cumulative-slip loss of support, the Christchurch collapse fraction rising between 0.25 g and 0.5 g, the Psycharis pulse behaviours and the PEER-style rocking-block statistics, the 3DEC four regimes with the 1 mm stability bound, the SimReady slope and five-sphere pyramid, the Box2D stack with a drift-and-energy criterion, and on the strength side the squat pier flipping from rocking to crushing at f_c = 1.21 MPa, the L-wall interlock margin, the Pulatsu sub-contact convergence, and the rigid-ring 46 % bias flag. The exit condition is a collapse fraction within the published bin for each population case and a mechanism flip where the literature reports one.

What stays out, by name: the FACE 2014-02 block wall (two dead, wind gust) until its 19-page report with height, bracing and gust is retrieved; the Medellín confined wall, which is an RC-framed system; the Colas and Bath retaining walls until their fill heights are in hand; any L'Aquila wall as a numeric case, since no source gives one wall's height, thickness, restraint and PGA together, although the ReLUIS captions — "iron ties avoided global overturning" and "collapse of the central zone of a wall determined by the horizontal action of the wooden beam" ([ReLUIS 2009](https://www.reluis.it/doc/pdf/Aquila/Report_masonry_building_1.pdf)) — are usable as tie-versus-no-tie flips on a generic 300–500 mm façade; and every Pavia, Noto and St Mark's variant that expects a fall.

## Conclusion

The field record and the solver literature turn out to validate opposite halves of the engine. The collapses that are fully documented are, without exception, friction-and-geometry failures — a cracked wall, a cut ring, a neoprene seat, a parapet, a drum stack — and their investigators reduced them to the same rigid-block checks the engine performs, so they confirm that the exact tier of the benchmark catalogue describes real structures and not just textbooks. The solver literature, by contrast, is almost entirely about what happens when the engine is asked to be more than that: declare a flow rule, choose a hinge convention, carry a strength, run on eight threads. The two halves meet in only one place, the bonded base joint of the Liberton wall, and that is where the stress-to-force path gets its sole field check — which argues for giving that one counterfactual more weight than its modest geometry suggests.

The negative controls deserve a final word because they invert the usual role of a test. Pavia, Noto, St Mark's and Pisa will pass trivially in any rigid-block engine, and that is the point: they state in the suite's own language that the famous tower collapses are not in scope, so that no future reader mistakes a passing suite for a claim about crushing or creep, and so that the day a compressive-strength law is added, four tests already exist whose expected answer should change. A suite that names what its engine cannot do is harder to over-trust than one that only lists what it can.
