# Known Answers That Anchor Rigid-Block Statics

A rigid-block statics engine can be pinned, today, to roughly thirty published known answers, and they sort cleanly into three tiers by how sharply the answer is defined. The first tier is exact and friction-independent: a block on an incline moves at **tan θ = min(μ, w/h)**, Housner's block uplifts at **a/g = b/h**, a free wall overturns at **λ = t/H**, the harmonic stack overhangs **(L/2)·H_n**, and the least-thickness semicircular arch is **t/r = 0.10748 with its haunch hinge at 54.48°** — numbers good to four significant figures that no engine has an excuse to miss. The second tier is numerical and flow-rule sensitive: the six Ferris & Tin-Loi walls (associative LP factors **0.64286 → 0.23964**, non-associative answers **0.6 % to 25.9 % lower**) and the Orduña & Lourenço hollow pile (non-associative answer provably inside **[0.427, 0.553]**) only become pass/fail once the engine declares whether it maximises, minimises or path-follows the load factor. The third tier is experimental, with bands of 3–10 %: Ochsendorf's plywood buttresses (theory within **≈6 %** of measured collapse, the monolithic answer **19–25 % high**), Peña's granite rocking blocks (measured slenderness within **3 %** of b/h), the Pavia tilt-table walls (**λ = 0.21–0.36**), the Minho dry granite piers (**37–41 kN** rocking at 100 kN axial) and Burgoyne's four 6.1 m retaining walls (two stand, two fall at 5.2 m of fill). Timber joints have no rigid-block collapse experiments at all; what exists are Eurocode 5 Johansen worked examples (**4.83 kN** screw, **12.9 kN** M16 bolt, **0.42 kN** nail withdrawal, all characteristic) that sit **7 % to 3×** below measured means, and mortar-joint strengths whose code values (**f_xk1 = 0.10 MPa, μ = 0.4**) are **3–5× and ~2.5×** below what couplet tests measure. A new engine should adopt the first tier plus one friction switch (the arch slides at φ = 21°, stands at 22°) before anything else, add Ferris & Tin-Loi Examples 1 and 4 and the pile as its flow-rule declaration, and treat the experiments as bracket tests with named mechanisms rather than equality assertions.

## Single blocks and stacks pin to four figures with no friction ambiguity

The simplest benchmarks are also the sharpest, because their answers are ratios of lengths. For a uniform block of width w and height h on a plane tilted to θ, sliding impends when tan θ equals μ and tipping when tan θ equals w/h; the lower angle governs, so the block tips first exactly when μ > w/h ([RAEng, Friction and sliding](https://raeng.org.uk/media/vuxfappb/6-friction-and-sliding.pdf)). The tipping half is a statement about where the normal resultant sits: "the object will tip when the resultant normal force N shifts off the end of the object" ([Baker & Haynes, Engineering Statics §9.2](https://engineeringstatics.org/Chapter_09-slipping-vs--tipping.html)). A 10 × 15 cm block with μ = 0.7 therefore tips at **33.69°** with sliding 1.3° away at **34.99°**; drop μ to 0.6 and it slides at 30.96° instead. Because both thresholds depend only on the ratio of tangential to normal gravity, tilting a base by θ is exactly equivalent to a mass-proportional horizontal load λW with **λ = tan θ** — the identity the MIT group uses to turn tilt tables into equivalent-static tests ([Block, DeJong & Ochsendorf 2006](https://web.mit.edu/masonry/papers/block_dejong_ochs_NNJ.pdf)). Every tilt-table result in this report doubles as a horizontal-load multiplier for that reason.

Housner's rocking block is the same criterion with a name. For a block of half-width b and half-height h on a tensionless, non-sliding base, "a necessary condition for motion to be initiated is that a/g > α" ([Housner 1963](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/47803)), stated exactly by later authors as **ü_g/g ≥ tan α = b/h** ([Voyagaki, Psycharis & Mylonakis 2012](https://www.iitk.ac.in/nicee/wcee/article/WCEE2012_0353.pdf)). Assert against tan α, not α: at α = 14° the two differ by 1.6 %. The experimental check is the LNEC blue-granite series, where specimens with h/b = 4 and 6 (widths 0.25 m and 0.17 m, height 1.0 m, 503 kg and 228 kg) gave measured slenderness angles of **0.235 and 0.163 rad against theoretical 0.242 and 0.168 rad**, "differences smaller than 3 %", with the shortfall attributed to "the rotation points are not located at the corners since the material is not perfectly rigid" ([Prieto, Peña, Lourenço & Lemos, SAHC 2006](https://repositorium.uminho.pt/server/api/core/bitstreams/82d0aa07-bc14-4ce2-8463-83630b179900/content)). The h/b = 8 block measured 0.154 rad against 0.119 and should not be used to calibrate anything. One warning travels with Housner: his static uplift condition is uncontested, but his finite-pulse overturning formulas are wrong — "under a half-sine pulse, a block overturns during its free vibration regime and not at the instant that the pulse expires" ([Makris & Roussos, PEER 1998-05](https://peer.berkeley.edu/publications/1998-05)) — so no dynamic overturning number from the 1963 paper belongs in a test.

Itasca publishes the incline problem as a four-regime classification with closed-form boundaries: on a 40° slope with a 10 m wide block, a 10 m tall block with φ = 40.1° is stable, a 5 m block with φ = 20° slides, a 25 m block with φ = 40° topples, and a 20 m block with φ = 30° does both ([3DEC Sliding and Toppling Blocks](https://docs.itascacg.com/itasca950/3dec/docproject/source/examples/SlidingBlock.html)). Its companion tetrahedral wedge on two planes (dips 40°/130° and 60°/220°, ρ = 2000 kg/m³, cohesionless) has an analytical critical friction angle of **33.36°** which the code reproduces at 33.12°, a 0.7 % error ([3DEC Sliding Wedge](https://docs.itascacg.com/itasca950/3dec/docproject/source/examples/SlidingWedge.html)). The textbook wedge adds a two-case switch: a 25° wedge under a 10 lb block with μ = 0.20 everywhere needs **9.35 lb to raise and 0.436 lb to hold**, because 25° exceeds the self-locking limit 2φ = 22.6°; raise μ to 0.25 and the same wedge stays put unaided ([Baker & Haynes §9.3](https://engineeringstatics.org/Chapter_09-block-and-wedge-friction.html)).

Stacks test unilateral contact without friction entering at all. Paterson & Zwick's formal model — blocks "can push, but not pull, one another", all forces vertical — makes balance "the feasibility of a collection of linear equations and inequalities with O(n) variables", and the harmonic stack of n blocks is balanced with overhang exactly ½H_n because "the center of mass of the topmost i blocks lies exactly above the right-hand edge of the (i+1)st block" ([Paterson & Zwick, arXiv:0710.2357](https://arxiv.org/abs/0710.2357)). The small-n values are 1/2, 3/4, 11/12, 25/24, 137/120 block lengths ([MathWorld, Book Stacking Problem](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/BookStackingProblem.html)). The sharper tests are the counterbalanced cases the same paper settles: three blocks reach overhang 1 L as an inverted 2-triangle, four reach (15 − 4√2)/8 ≈ **1.16789 L**, yet "the next inverted triangle is already unbalanced", the 4-diamond stands and the 5-diamond falls — and "inverted triangles show that simple center-of-mass considerations are not enough to determine the balance of stacks" ([Paterson & Zwick](https://arxiv.org/abs/0710.2357)). An engine that redistributes contact forces correctly across multi-support interfaces will pass the 3-triangle/4-diamond pair; one that checks centroids will not. The harmonic stack is knife-edge, so test it two-sided (stands at offsets × 0.99, falls at × 1.01) and perturb one block to identify which sub-stack rotates. A uniform-offset stack of n blocks with constant step d follows from the same centre-of-mass condition and stands iff d ≤ L/n, with the top n − 1 blocks rotating about the bottom block's edge when exceeded; this is a derivation from the MathWorld balancing rule rather than a published number, and the same rule governs a 2-D corbel. Corbelled domes are explicitly not covered: ring compression lets them stand where 2-D corbelling theory says they fall ([Rovero & Tonietti 2014](https://flore.unifi.it/handle/2158/820494)).

## Walls, piers and buttresses: experiments agree with no-tension theory, not with monoliths

A free-standing rigid wall of thickness t and height H is a Housner block with b/h = t/H, so under a mass-proportional lateral load it overturns at **λ = t/H** about its toe and slides at λ = μ, the smaller governing; this is the mechanism-A base case every façade calculation reduces to. Building the same wall from courses changes nothing if every joint shares one μ — the lowest joint is always the critical hinge, since the part above any joint at height z has multiplier t/(H − z) ≥ t/H — so a coursed-wall test is really a check that the engine opens the base joint and no other. The Pavia tilt-table programme is the experimental version: 42 configurations of 1:5-scale dry-stacked marble walls with return walls, openings and overburden, where "the collapse multiplier was calculated from the inclination angle of the platform that caused collapse" ([Restrepo Vélez, Magenes & Griffith 2014](https://iris.unipv.it/handle/11571/461976)). The accessible per-configuration values are reprinted by Bui et al., together with the block friction band (0.67–0.77 measured, 0.67 used) and unit weight 2,680 kg/m³: façade overturning gave **λ = 0.291 (L/H 1.088), 0.362 and 0.352 (L/H 0.816), 0.213 (L/H 1.633)**, with 3DEC at 0.287/0.326/0.326/0.221; the trapezoidal "mechanism G" with vertical plus diagonal tear lines gave 0.254/0.226/0.244 for three repeats at L/H 1.50, 0.349 at 1.09 and 0.208 at 1.77 ([Bui, Limam, Sarhosis & Hjiaj 2017](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/144603/3/BUI-ALI-VASILIS-MANUSCRIP-REVISED-V17new_Final.pdf)). The calibration lesson in that table is more valuable than any single number: the D'Ayala (2003) closed forms predicted 0.438–0.545 for the same façades, **40–67 % high**, because "torsional friction along the sloped crack line is not taken into account", while Vaculik's 2010 formulation landed within 0–11 % ([Bui et al. 2017](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/144603/3/BUI-ALI-VASILIS-MANUSCRIP-REVISED-V17new_Final.pdf)). An engine should be validated against the measured multipliers, never the arching closed forms, and a mechanism-G test is in effect a test of whether bed-joint torsional friction exists in the contact model. The tightest wall experiment of all is the Casapulla–Maione tuff corner: five tilt tests, mean **λ = 0.33 with CV 2.7 %**, μ = 0.62, density 1.23 t/m³, failing by a mixed rocking–sliding and horizontal-flexure mode; a Minho micro-model with the same zero-tension, zero-cohesion, μ = 0.62 interfaces gave 0.38, and friction angle was the one parameter with "a very significant impact" ([Colombo, Fernandes, Savalle & Lourenço 2021](https://www.canadamasonrydesigncentre.com/wp-content/uploads/14th_symposium/021-Colombo.pdf)).

In-plane, the dry-joint piers at Minho give force thresholds at fixed axial load. The Lourenço et al. walls are 1.0 × 1.0 × 0.2 m of 0.1 × 0.2 × 0.2 m sawn stone, pre-compressed to 30, 100, 200 and 250 kN, failing by "diagonal stepped crack initiated from the top right-hand corner" and crushing at the higher loads ([Bui et al. 2017](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/144603/3/BUI-ALI-VASILIS-MANUSCRIP-REVISED-V17new_Final.pdf)); for SW30 the rigid-block limit analysis is the asymptote of a DIANA curve that reaches **16.3 kN at 15 mm** with μ = 0.66 ([Orduña & Lourenço 2005, Part II](https://repositorium.uminho.pt/bitstreams/1b6aa5d7-2879-4a6f-a6d5-6635709a3e0f/download)). The Vasconcelos granite walls (1.2 × 1.0 × 0.2 m, blocks 0.15 × 0.20 × 0.20 m, 25.7 kN/m³) rocked at **36.9/−40.7 kN under 100 kN**, 62.8/−67.8 kN under 175 kN and 86.3/−93.0 kN under 250 kN with toe crushing, and the rocking formula H_r = (l²tσ/2h)(1 − σ/0.85f_c) with f_c = 73 MPa sits "remarkably close" as an upper bound ([Vasconcelos & Lourenço 2006](https://repositorium.uminho.pt/server/api/core/bitstreams/9b4098a4-342f-4b9b-b49c-08c1f155334b/content)). Orduña's infinite-strength limit analysis of the 250 kN wall gives **95.2 kN** against a two-direction mean of 91.6 kN ([Orduña & Lourenço 2005, Part II](https://repositorium.uminho.pt/bitstreams/1b6aa5d7-2879-4a6f-a6d5-6635709a3e0f/download)), so a no-crushing engine should land a few percent above the measured peaks at high axial load and within about 5 % at 100 kN where rocking is pure. The regime boundary itself is pinned by the Ispra brick pair: 1.0 m long walls at 0.60 MPa axial stress failed by diagonal stepped sliding at **75 kN when 1.35 m tall** and by flexure at **65 kN when 2.0 m tall** ([Materials 2021 review, PMC8200034](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8200034/)).

Ochsendorf's buttresses are the case that separates a rigid-monolith engine from a no-tension one. Conventional analysis assumes a buttress overturns as one body; "the current paper demonstrates that this approach is clearly unsafe" because a stress-free fracture forms from the loaded-side base corner and sheds a wedge, cutting capacity by **20–30 %** ([Ochsendorf, Hernando & Huerta 2004](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1061/(ASCE)1076-0431(2004)10:3(88)?fields=title,authors,year,venue,abstract,externalIds)). The thesis worked example — 12 m tall, 3 m wide, 1.5 m deep at 2000 kg/m³, 100 kN vertical load, thrust at 8 m — gives a monolithic **H_s = 234 kN**, a fractured **H_u ≈ 178 kN** with the fracture reaching **e = 5.2 m** (ξ = 0.65), and a sliding capacity of 317 kN at μ = 0.7 that never governs ([Ochsendorf 2002 thesis](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/bitstream/1810/244820/1/Ochsendorf25710.pdf)). The experiments are plywood-block models 72.8 cm tall and 18.2 cm wide with sand-glued faces to prevent sliding: the rectangular buttress collapsed at **710 gf against a fractured prediction of 667 gf and a monolithic 878 gf**; with 500 g on top, 920 gf against 955 and 1128; the T-section at 980 against 1023 and 1360, and 1230 against 1279 and 1596 — "within approximately 6 % of the actual collapse load" in every case, with failure "by opening of the joints between masonry blocks" while "the buttress remains essentially vertical" until it separates into an effective mass and an ineffective wedge ([Ochsendorf 2002 thesis, §3.8](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/bitstream/1810/244820/1/Ochsendorf25710.pdf)). The assumptions matter here more than anywhere else: this is a linear-compression continuum result (the fracture forms where the resultant leaves the middle third), so a discrete-block engine will approach H_u only as course height shrinks and will otherwise land between H_u and H_s. The right assertion is a bracket — capacity below the monolithic value, mechanism a base hinge with a detached lower wedge, no sliding — not an equality. Leaning reduces capacity linearly, about **10 kN per degree** for the Old Goa buttress whose fractured capacity is 69 kN against a solid 112 kN ([MIT OCW 4.448, Lecture 4](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/4-448-analysis-of-historic-structures-fall-2004/aa1e9e8eb38dc698f33c66d1dbe205c2_lec4_masonry3.pdf)).

Dry-stone retaining walls bring a soil driver but the cleanest stand/fall discriminator in the literature. Burgoyne's 1834 Kingstown trial built four 6.1 m granite walls of equal volume: A (1.02 m uniform, battered 1:5) and B (1.63 m tapering to 0.41 m, vertical back) reached full height, B moving 63 mm at the top; C (B's section with the batter at the rear) and D (1.02 m uniform, vertical) "collapsed when the backfill reached a height of about 5.2 m", D "toppling from base" with a 0.45 m overhang beforehand and C bursting at 1.5–1.7 m ([Brady & Kavanagh, TRL550](https://www.stonewalls.ch/system/files/2025-04/2002%20Analysis%20stability%20masonry%20retaining%20walls.pdf); [Mundell et al. 2010](https://purehost.bath.ac.uk/ws/files/305502/stbu163-003.pdf)). UDEC with granite at 2270 kg/m³, block friction 45°, loose fill at 1390–1550 kg/m³ and φ = 28° "reproduced this behaviour", and the parametric runs found block sliding "relatively small compared to those generated by overturning of the base about its toe" while a vertical joint parallel to the face could switch a stable wall to overturning ([TRL550](https://www.stonewalls.ch/system/files/2025-04/2002%20Analysis%20stability%20masonry%20retaining%20walls.pdf)). The structural parameter to vary is therefore bond pattern, not μ. Bath's four instrumented 2.5 m limestone walls extend this to surcharge: peaks of **110, 75, 80 and 84 kN** against a monolithic limit-equilibrium prediction of 130, 53, 87 and 132 kN, walls 1–2 toppling and walls 3–4 (44–46 % voidage) failing by an internal bulge "because of bulging … also a visible bursting" that a monolith cannot represent ([Mundell et al. 2010](https://purehost.bath.ac.uk/ws/files/305502/stbu163-003.pdf)).

## Arches are the four-significant-figure anchor, once the radius is named

No other rigid-block benchmark is as precisely published as the Couplet–Heyman problem, and no other has as many ways to be subtly wrong. For a semicircular arch of centreline radius r under self-weight with radial joints, the reference answer is Milankovitch's: **η = t/r = 0.107478, haunch hinge at β = 54.484° from the crown, dimensionless thrust h = 0.620881** (ĥ = H/γdr² = 0.0667311) ([Cocchetti, Colasante & Rizzi 2011, Table 7](https://aisberg.unibg.it/retrieve/472995cf-86c8-4292-9661-8cc57ef17bd9/PaperCCRAMR11-Accepted.pdf)). Heyman's often-quoted **0.105965 with the hinge at 58.8293°** is a distinct, slightly unconservative variant that puts the half-arch weight on the centreline; Makris & Alexakis show it "remains unconservative regardless the stereotomy exercised on the arch" ([Makris & Alexakis 2015](https://www.ijbe.net/issues/volumes/item/download/104_225d6c04d4d044dd4b08acc37f969f22)). Vertical joints raise the requirement to **0.10946 at 54.923°** ([same source](https://www.ijbe.net/issues/volumes/item/download/104_225d6c04d4d044dd4b08acc37f969f22)), and Couplet's 1730 answer of 0.101 with hinges at 45° is history, not a target. The normalisation trap is real: Iannuzzo's rigid-block LP reproduces the same arch as **0.1135818** because he divides by the intrados radius, and his 500-element solution brackets it at [0.11357, 0.11359] ([Iannuzzo 2017 thesis, §6.5](https://www.fedoa.unina.it/11732/1/Iannuzzo_Antonino_29.pdf)) — 0.1075/(1 − 0.1075/2) is 0.1136, so a test must state which radius it means. Discretisation error is small and known: a 3DEC model converged to Milankovitch within "± 1.5° and ± 0.0005 · R", the residual coming from the fact that a hinge must fall on a joint ([Forgács, Sarhosis & Bagi 2017](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/144597/3/Manuscript_ForgacsSarhosisBagi_EngStr_revised_marked_20161218.pdf)). Either place a joint at 54.48° or assert 0.1075 ± 0.001.

The same paper tabulates all three formulations at 10° steps of half-embrace, so segmental arches pin as well as the semicircle. For α ≤ 80° the formulations agree in t/r to better than 1 % (α = 60°: **0.02285, hinge 39.46°, h = 0.8251**); from 100° upward they diverge, reaching 0.5739 (Heyman) against 0.6349 (Milankovitch) at α = 140°, so an over-complete arch test must name its formulation ([Cocchetti et al. 2011](https://aisberg.unibg.it/retrieve/472995cf-86c8-4292-9661-8cc57ef17bd9/PaperCCRAMR11-Accepted.pdf)). The purely rotational solution exists only up to a half-opening of **151.7°**, where the thrust falls to zero ([Cocchetti & Rizzi 2024](https://re.public.polimi.it/retrieve/e3e524b4-a174-43e3-97f6-941a2092481d/Cocchetti-Rizzi-STRUCTURES-2024(66)-%5BFinite-friction-least-thickness-self-standing-domains-of-symmetric-circular-masonry-arches%5D.pdf)).

Friction gives the arch family its one clean switch. For the least-thickness semicircular arch "the minimum necessary angle of friction that prevents shear sliding is equal to 21.57°" at the springing, and the DEM confirms it: "a shear sliding failure mechanism observed when friction angle was 21°. In the case that the friction angle increases to 22°, the arch is standing" ([Forgács, Sarhosis & Bagi 2017](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/144597/3/Manuscript_ForgacsSarhosisBagi_EngStr_revised_marked_20161218.pdf)). That is μ = 0.384 falls, μ = 0.404 stands — a two-run test. Any μ ≥ 0.5 reproduces the no-sliding answer for this arch, and **μ ≥ 1.41527** excludes sliding for every opening up to 142.5° ([Cocchetti & Rizzi 2020](https://re.public.polimi.it/retrieve/e0c31c10-4b89-4599-e053-1705fe0aef77/Cocchetti-Rizzi-JOTA-2020(187)-%5BStatic-upper-lower-thrust-and-kinematic-work-balance-stationarity-for-least-thickness-circular-masonry-arch-optimization%5D.pdf)).

Lateral load has closed-form multipliers of its own. Iannuzzo's analytical solutions for a circular arch under self-weight plus a mass-proportional horizontal load, with his LP brackets alongside, are **λ = 0.340 for a round arch of intrados radius 1 and thickness 0.25** (hinges at 0°, 47.47°, 108.75°, 170.04°; LP [0.334, 0.340]), 0.392 for a 150° arch with t = 0.15, and 0.549 for a 120° arch with t = 0.10 ([Iannuzzo 2017 thesis, §6.6](https://www.fedoa.unina.it/11732/1/Iannuzzo_Antonino_29.pdf)). The MIT equivalent-static result is the complementary pin for a discrete arch: sixteen voussoirs, embrace 157.5°, t/R = 0.15, collapses "at a constant horizontal acceleration of 0.34·g" with four hinges, and the same arch on buttresses 1.5 span tall and 0.5 span wide at 0.13 g, with the authors' caveat that "local crushing and sliding have not been evaluated" ([Block, DeJong & Ochsendorf 2006](https://web.mit.edu/masonry/papers/block_dejong_ochs_NNJ.pdf)). Spreading supports supply the one arch experiment with a fully specified rigid-voussoir model: sixteen concrete voussoirs of 50 mm radial thickness at mean radius 220 mm (t/R = 0.23) collapsed at a span increase of **15.4 %** against Ochsendorf's static prediction of 16.9 % and a kinematic 16.53 %, after a three-hinge pre-collapse state (crown extrados, two intrados hinges) whose hinges migrate as the span grows ([Coccia, Di Carlo & Rinaldi 2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40091-015-0101-x); [Block, DeJong & Ochsendorf 2006](https://web.mit.edu/masonry/papers/block_dejong_ochs_NNJ.pdf)).

Point-loaded bridges are mechanism benchmarks, not load benchmarks, for a voussoir-only engine. The UPC laboratory pair makes the point numerically: BA1 (span 3.2 m, rise 0.65 m, 14 cm ring, 1 m wide) and BA2 (rise 1.6 m) failed at **60 and 95 kN** under a quarter-span load, but the ring-alone limit analysis gives only 15 and 16.1 kN; spandrel walls and ties supply the rest, and the four hinges appeared in the order loaded section, near springing, crown at 80–90 % of peak, then the far springing ([Roca & Molins, ARCH'04](https://caminstech.upc.edu/sites/default/files/Experiments%20on%20Arch%20Bridges.pdf)). Prestwood — a 6.55 m span, 0.22 m single brick ring, 0.165 m of fill at the crown, 3.8 m wide, loaded on a 0.3 m strip at quarter span — collapsed at **228 kN** by four hinges ([Drosopoulos, Stavroulakis & Massalas](https://arch-bridges.fzu.edu.cn/__local/8/83/32/FFD47B4C2992F51ED7390BB691E_B7B56259_B3646.pdf?e=.pdf); [Page 1987, TRRL RR117](https://trid.trb.org/view/283016)). Deep arches are worse: Bargower derived "only 8 % of its strength from the barrel" ([Sarhosis, De Santis & de Felice](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/146294/2/SARHOS~1.pdf)), so its 5600 kN says nothing about a rigid-block solver. Use these for hinge order and load position only.

## The flow rule decides the number, so the engine must declare its convention

Everything above is friction-independent or carries a single friction switch. The moment a mechanism involves sliding on more than one joint, Coulomb friction's non-associativity makes the limit load non-unique, and the benchmark literature splits by convention. Livesley framed it at the origin of the field: with Coulomb friction "the failure mechanism computed by the algorithm will not necessarily satisfy the normality rule. The corresponding limit load may therefore be an over-estimate of the true failure load, even though it is computed by a lower-bound (equilibrium) approach" ([Livesley 1978](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.1620121207)). Drucker's bounds frame the range: the frictional limit load lies above the zero-friction answer and below both the no-sliding answer and the associative (dilatant) answer ([iMechanica note on Drucker 1954](https://www.imechanica.org/sites/default/files/plasticity_and_friction_imechanica.pdf)).

The Ferris & Tin-Loi walls are the best-documented known-answer suite in the field precisely because they publish all three conventions on the same six problems. Blocks are dimensionless 4 × 1.75 (half blocks 2 × 1.75), φ = tan⁻¹ 0.65, each full block carrying unit self-weight and a horizontal live load α, with dilatancy φ₀ = φ for the associative LP and φ₀ = 0 for the MCP and MPEC ([Tin-Loi & Ferris, TR 99-01](https://minds.wisconsin.edu/bitstream/handle/1793/64402/99-01.pdf)). Their Table 1 reads:

| Example | Blocks × contacts | LP α (associative) | MCP α | MPEC α (non-assoc.) | MPEC vs LP |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 33 × 83 | 0.64286 | 0.64285 | 0.63898 | 0.6 % |
| 2 (solid wall, 5 × 11) | 55 × 141 | 0.58000 | 0.56368 | 0.55742 | 4.1 % |
| 3 (one opening) | 46 × 102 | 0.37383 | 0.31078 | 0.31078 | 20.3 % |
| 4 (two openings) | 55 × 116 | 0.33195 | 0.26374 | 0.26374 | 25.9 % |
| 5 (stacked openings) | 61 × 120 | 0.23964 | 0.21584 | 0.20863 | 14.9 % |
| 6 (four openings) | 146 × 345 | 0.34782 | 0.29725 | 0.29577 | 17.6 % |

([Tin-Loi & Ferris, TR 99-01, Table 1](https://minds.wisconsin.edu/bitstream/handle/1793/64402/99-01.pdf)). "The assumption of associativity (in the LP problems) produced higher collapse loads (up to about 25.9 % higher for Example 4)." Example 1 is the control where the flow rule barely matters; Example 4 is the discriminator. An associative referee must hit the LP column; a non-associative engine should land between the MPEC and LP columns, remembering the MPEC is a local-minimum heuristic and lower values are theoretically possible. The exact layout of Example 1 is lost (its figure is blank in the surviving PDF) and Baggio & Trovalusci's original geometries are only "very similar", so Examples 2–6, whose mechanisms are drawn, are the reproducible ones. Gilbert, Casapulla & Ahmed's iterative-LP method was validated on "problems from the literature" that are almost certainly this set, but its tables are paywalled ([Gilbert et al. 2006](https://iris.unina.it/handle/11588/300870)).

Orduña & Lourenço's hollow pile is the one 3-D case with a closed bracket. Thirty-six or fewer dry blocks of 0.2 × 0.2 × 0.4 m form a 0.6 × 0.8 × 1.2 m pile at 20 kN/m³ with μ = 0.7, loaded horizontally in proportion to weight along the 0.8 m side: for the observed mechanism the load factor must lie between **0.427** (hinge reaction on one interface) and **0.553**; DIANA FEM gives 0.479, load-path-following limit analysis **0.500**, and minimising the load factor by the Ferris & Tin-Loi procedure returns exactly the lower bound 0.427, "10.8 % lower than the factor obtained by FEM" ([Orduña & Lourenço 2005, Part II](https://repositorium.uminho.pt/bitstreams/1b6aa5d7-2879-4a6f-a6d5-6635709a3e0f/download)). Their two out-of-plane walls show the practical danger of the minimum-α convention: a 1.053 × 0.630 × 0.071 m wall of 0.081 × 0.210 × 0.07 m blocks supported on one vertical edge gives FEM 0.210 and load-path 0.216 but min-α **0.127, "40 %"** off with a different mechanism; the 1.260 m wall supported on both edges gives 0.260 / 0.285 / 0.193 ([same source](https://repositorium.uminho.pt/bitstreams/1b6aa5d7-2879-4a6f-a6d5-6635709a3e0f/download)). Their general finding — the load-path answer "always gives slightly larger ultimate load factors than the FEM calculations" because the piecewise-linear yield surface overestimates strength at some stress states — is the expected sign of error for any LP-based engine.

Two more facts about the numerical landscape shape expectations. First, there is no maintained rigid-block benchmark collection; what exists are blind-prediction exercises on shaking-table specimens where 3-D rigid-block limit analysis was one method among several ([SERA-AIMS cross vault, IRIS UNINA](https://iris.unina.it/handle/11588/838331)), plus de-facto suites inherited from the papers above. Second, codes that look like reference tools mostly are not: 3DEC's masonry examples (arch bridge creeping at 6000 kN, Flemish wall, flying buttress) are demonstrations with joint stiffness and no comparator ([3DEC Highway Loading](https://docs.itascacg.com/itasca950/3dec/docproject/source/examples/HighwayLoading.html)), the COMPAS CRA repository ships reproducible inputs (a 20-block arch of span 10, height 5, thickness 0.5, μ = 0.7; three stacked unit cubes rotated 20°) but its reference answers live in a paywalled paper ([compas_cra examples](https://github.com/BlockResearchGroup/compas_cra/tree/main/docs/examples); [Kao et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cad.2022.103216)), and Chrono's masonry work uses associative friction by construction, so it will match the LP column, not the MPEC ([Beatini, Royer-Carfagni & Tasora](https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/en/publications/a-regularized-non-smooth-contact-dynamics-approach-for-architectu/)).

## Timber fasteners and mortar joints have worked examples, not collapse experiments

For bonded joints the literature offers joint-level strengths rather than assembly-level stand/fall tests, and almost all of it is characteristic (5 % fractile) rather than mean. The fully numbered Eurocode 5 examples that are freely downloadable are steel-to-timber: an 8 mm × 60 mm wood screw (f_u = 410 MPa) through an 8 mm plate into GL30c (ρ_k = 390 kg/m³) at 45° to grain gives M_y,Rk = 27.4 N·m, f_h,α,k = 23.82 MPa and failure modes c/d/e of 9.91/**4.83**/5.26 kN, so 4.83 kN characteristic per shear plane; a 4.0 × 40 mm ring-shank nail through a 5 mm plate gives **1.607 kN**; an M16 bolt (f_u = 360 MPa) in double shear through a 90 mm member gives **12.9 kN per bolt** in mode k ([Swedish Wood, Design of timber structures Vol. 3, Examples 4.1–4.3](https://www.svenskttra.se/siteassets/5-publikationer/pdfer/sw-design-of-timber-structures-vol3-2022.pdf)). The screw and bolt values re-derive exactly from EN 1995-1-1 eq. (8.10); the nail value does not (a session recomputation gives ≈1.48 kN), so treat 1.607 kN as published rather than reproduced. Axial capacities come from worked withdrawal examples: a 3.1 mm ring-shank nail with 55 mm penetration into ρ_k = 350 kg/m³ timber gives f_ax,k = 2.45 MPa and **F_ax,Rk = 0.42 kN** ([Structural Basics, Tensile Capacity of Nails](https://www.structuralbasics.com/?p=6714)); a 2.5 mm screw with 27.5 mm effective thread gives **1.74 kN** — but by the pre-2008 ETA formula, which differs from the EN 1995-1-1:2004/A1:2008 §8.7.2 form, so an implementer must pick one and say so ([Structural Basics, Tensile Capacity of Screws](https://www.structuralbasics.com/tensile-capacity-of-screws-eurocode-tutorial/)). Bearing perpendicular to grain is a stress check: C24 has **f_c,90,k = 2.5 MPa** ([Swedish Wood Vol. 3, Example 3.2](https://www.svenskttra.se/siteassets/5-publikationer/pdfer/sw-design-of-timber-structures-vol3-2022.pdf)), the contact length extends 30 mm at each free end, and k_c,90 is 1.5 for solid timber on discrete supports, 1.75 for glulam, else 1.0 ([FIN EC help](https://finesoftware.eu/help/truss4/en/compression-perpendicular-to-the-grain-in-supports-01/)).

How far those characteristic numbers sit below reality is itself a known answer. Blass & Colling evaluated 1,588 dowelled-joint tests and found the EN 14358 characteristic ratio test/F_v,Rk to be **1.074 for timber-to-timber and 1.073 for steel-to-timber** with the 1.05/1.15 code factors removed — "slightly conservative" — with the gap widening with dowel diameter, a further "25 %" surplus in two-hinge modes from a slenderness effect, and measured dowel yield moments 1.5–3× the EC5 value from nominal f_u at 12–20 mm (a 16 mm bolt: 494 N·m measured against 146 N·m from f_u,k) ([Blass & Colling, INTER 48-07-03](https://publikationen.bibliothek.kit.edu/1000049999/3752771)). For slender nails the ultimate load runs roughly 3.3× the yield-model plastic load because of rope effect and hardening, with the yield load itself predicted within 6–28 % ([ASCE J. Mater. Civ. Eng. 1993, abstract](https://ascelibrary.com/doi/10.1061/%28ASCE%290899-1561%281993%295%3A2%28226%29)) — a second-hand figure, but the direction is unambiguous. A realism-first engine should therefore store the bare Johansen plastic-hinge load with a full-plastic f_y·d³/6 yield moment and real steel strength, and benchmark the onset of plastic slip rather than the peak. No published rigid-block-style experiment on a timber frame or lap joint collapsing was found; frame-level cases (three-hinged portal thrust PL/4h, overhanging beam tipping when the overhang exceeds the back span) are textbook statics, and the propped cantilever is a deliberate negative benchmark because its reactions need joint compliance a rigid-block solver does not have.

Mortar joints show a larger code-to-mean gap. EN 1996-1-1's recommended characteristic values for clay units in general-purpose mortar are **f_xk1 = 0.10 MPa** (bending about the bed joint), f_xk2 = 0.20–0.40 MPa, **f_vk0 = 0.30 MPa** for M10–M20 mortar, and f_vk = f_vk0 + 0.4σ_d capped at 0.065 f_b, halved for unfilled perpends ([EN 1996-1-1 §3.6, DSTU adoption](https://dnaop.com/html/60058_6.html); [§3.6.2](https://dnaop.com/html/60058_5.html)). Measured means on Australian clay brick with 1:1:6 mortar are a different world: flexural bond is **2.06× direct tension** (COV 31.5 %) and shear bond **1.34× flexural** ([Gooch, Masia, Stewart & Lam 2023](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/87642/)); direct tensile bond runs 0.15–0.25 MPa, couplet shear bond 0.58–1.04 MPa, and the residual friction coefficient is **1.04–1.16 at 0.22 MPa normal stress falling to 0.60–0.91 at 1.0 MPa**, fitted as μ = 1.12·e^(−0.35σ_n) for extruded brick with COV 0.13 — against the code's 0.4 ([Gooch, Masia, Stewart & Spadari 2025](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/87638/1/Experimental%20characterisation%20of%20the%20friction%20coefficient%20of%20mortar%20bed%20joints%20in%20clay-brick%20masonry.pdf)). A 93-joint bond-wrench survey of a full concrete-brick wall gave a mean of **0.52 MPa with COV 0.43**, while face-shell-bedded blockwork managed only 0.05–0.07 MPa ([Gooch et al., 15th CMS 2025](https://www.canadamasonrydesigncentre.com/wp-content/uploads/15th_symposium/019-Gooch.pdf)). The weak end is lime mortar on handmade brick: triplets gave **c = 0.16 MPa and φ = 33.71°** ([Segura, Pelà, Saloustros & Roca, UPC](https://upcommons.upc.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/77631472-a1eb-4bc2-b2ad-5bfcb4bd5f02/content)). Friction falling with normal stress means a single Coulomb μ is an approximation; a benchmark that pins μ should state the normal stress it applies at. The single-joint bond-wrench test is exact and small — the joint opens at M = f_x·Z plus the axial correction, which for a 230 × 110 mm bed (Z = 4.64 × 10⁻⁴ m³) is 46 N·m at the code's 0.10 MPa and 241 N·m at the 0.52 MPa measured mean — and it is the only place the engine's stress-to-force conversion is exercised, because every friction-and-geometry benchmark above is strength-free.

Assembly-level bonded tests exist but need care. Vermeltfoort's eighteen identical walls — nine courses of stretcher bond, 562.5 mm high, on 2.8 m prefabricated lintels — failed at a mean shear of **31 kN under four-point and 24.4 kN under one-point loading**, with ultimate 15 % above first cracking, and the author's arch-foot check with τ = 0.35 + 0.65σ over a 250 mm contact gives a 15 kN reaction and a 19 kN lintel tension; most walls failed by bed-joint shear near the supports, so the assertion is first slip at the lintel–brick joint, not midspan tension ([Vermeltfoort, 11th CMS 2009](https://www.canadamasonrydesigncentre.com/wp-content/uploads/11th_symposium/B8-2.pdf)). Derakhshan's six full-scale one-way URM walls give the rigid-body correction for cracked walls: uncracked strength predicted "within 24 %", and the cracked rocking resistance "on average, 83 % of the predicted value based upon the mechanics of rigid body rocking" ([Derakhshan, Griffith & Ingham, Adelaide repository](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/e1aeaedc-e903-4459-a590-e6fb05d1949e)). Diagonal compression is a negative benchmark for a rigid-brick engine unless the bond permits a stair-step: five Flemish-bond lime-mortar panels failed at 115–179 kN, but "the length of the bricks used herein hindered the formation of a stair-stepped crack and necessarily required the failure of bricks in tension" ([Segura et al., UPC](https://upcommons.upc.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/77631472-a1eb-4bc2-b2ad-5bfcb4bd5f02/content)), which an unbreakable-brick model cannot reproduce.

## Adopt the exact anchors first, declare the flow rule second, bracket the experiments third

The adoption order follows from how tightly each answer is pinned and what it isolates. The first tier is seven exact, strength-free problems that between them exercise unilateral contact, moment equilibrium about an edge, redistribution across multiple supports, and the Coulomb cone — without any flow-rule or compliance ambiguity: the incline crossover (33.69° tip versus 34.99° slide at μ = 0.7, w/h = 2/3); Housner's block at a/g = b/h for the three granite geometries 0.25/0.17/0.12; the free wall at λ = t/H with the base joint as the only hinge; the harmonic four-stack at 25/24 L together with the inverted 3-triangle falling and the 4-diamond standing; the Milankovitch arch at t/r = 0.1075 ± 0.001 with its hinge within one joint of 54.48°; Iannuzzo's lateral arch at λ = 0.340; and the 3DEC incline regimes. Tolerances of 1–3 % on loads and 0.3–0.5 ° on angles separate correct from incorrect behaviour everywhere in this tier, and the knife-edge cases need a two-sided bracket rather than an equality. The second tier is the friction switches, still exact: the arch standing at φ = 22° and sliding at 21°, the 25° wedge self-locking at μ = 0.25 but not 0.20, the 3DEC wedge at φ_crit = 33.36°. These are two-run tests whose pass criterion is a flip in mechanism, and they prove the Coulomb cone is wired to the right joint.

The third tier is where the engine must make a declaration before any assertion can be written. Ferris & Tin-Loi Example 1 (LP 0.64286, MPEC 0.63898) and Example 4 (LP 0.33195, MPEC 0.26374) bracket the flow-rule sensitivity from negligible to 26 %, and the Orduña pile demands an answer inside [0.427, 0.553] with 0.500 as the load-path reference. An engine that maximises an associative LP must match the LP column; one that minimises under zero dilatancy must match the MPEC column and should expect to under-predict Orduña's one-edge wall by 40 %; one with a physically motivated path-following or non-smooth contact solver should land between the columns and near 0.50 on the pile. Which of these the engine is must be written into the test's name.

Only then do experiments earn their place, and they should be adopted in order of band width with the mechanism as the primary assertion and the load as a bracket. Ochsendorf's rectangular model buttress (measured 710 gf and 920 gf, bracketed by H_u 667/955 gf below and H_s 878/1128 gf above) is first, because it falsifies the monolithic answer by 19–25 % and the pass condition — capacity below H_s, base hinge with a detached lower wedge, no sliding — holds for any course height. Next come Peña's granite blocks (α within 3 % for h/b = 4 and 6), the Casapulla–Maione corner (0.33 ± 0.01 at μ = 0.62), the Vasconcelos WS.100 pier (37–41 kN) and WS.250 (86–93 kN against a 95.2 kN rigid upper bound), the Pavia façades S7–S10 run at both ends of the 0.67–0.77 friction band with monotonic λ(μ) asserted, the spreading arch at 15.4–16.9 % span increase, and Burgoyne's A–D stand/fall split once a soil-pressure driver exists. Bonded-joint anchors come last and are joint-level: the bond-wrench opening moment f_x·Z, the EC5 screw at 4.83 kN in mode d, the Blass & Colling 1.074 ratio as the characteristic-to-measured correction, and Vermeltfoort's 24–31 kN composite lintels as the first assembly test with cohesive beds.

Several widely cited numbers should be excluded by name. Housner's half-sine and rectangular-pulse overturning accelerations are wrong. The D'Ayala arching closed forms over-predict tilt-table façades by 40–67 %. Corbelled domes are not 2-D corbelling benchmarks. Deep-fill bridges (Bargower, 8 % from the barrel) and spandrel-stiffened laboratory arches (UPC's 60 and 95 kN) are not voussoir-only targets. Flemish-bond diagonal compression requires brick fracture. The propped cantilever requires compliance. And no benchmark here with the single exception of bond strength exercises a stress-to-force conversion: friction is dimensionless, cohesion is zero, compressive strength is infinite, and the buttress kern criterion is a shape rule, not a strength — so the geometry benchmarks will pass a 100× unit error in the strength path untouched, and the bonded-joint tier is the only place that error can be caught.

## Conclusion

The striking pattern across the five families is that the sharpest answers are not the "realistic" ones. The arch and stack problems pin to four figures because they assume infinite compressive strength, zero tension and either infinite or irrelevant friction; every step toward realism — finite friction on multiple joints, finite compressive strength, cohesion, joint compliance — widens the answer from a number into a convention-dependent band, and the experiments confirm the band rather than the number (Ochsendorf within 6 %, Peña within 3 %, Pavia repeats spread ±6 %, Bath walls 0.71–1.18 of a monolithic prediction). A test suite built in that order therefore does something more useful than verify arithmetic: each added assumption has a benchmark whose answer changes when it is relaxed, so the suite documents which physics the engine actually carries.

Two consequences follow for a new engine. First, the flow-rule declaration is not a detail to defer — Ferris & Tin-Loi Example 4 and the Orduña one-edge wall show that the same geometry yields answers 26–40 % apart under conventions that are each defensible, and a suite that does not fix the convention cannot distinguish a solver bug from a modelling choice. Second, the literature's gaps are where the engine will be most on its own: there is no open closed-form point-load collapse for a bare voussoir arch, no published two-block or four-block portal with both flow rules, no timber-joint assembly tested to collapse as rigid bodies, and no tabulated per-configuration tilt angles for most of the Pavia programme. Those are the scenarios the engine will have to generate its own oracle for, and the exact tier above is what makes that oracle trustworthy.
